Key Definitions

Terms to Know Cold

Bond
A long-term debt security requiring the issuer to make specified payments to the bondholder.
Issuer
The company or government that borrows by selling the bond.
Bondholder
The investor who lends money by buying the bond and receives promised payments.
Face value
The amount repaid at maturity; also called par value, nominal value, maturity value, or redemption value.
Coupon
The periodic interest payment paid to the bondholder.
Coupon rate
The annual rate applied to face value to calculate the coupon payment.
Maturity
The date when the issuer repays face value and the bond expires.
Yield to maturity
The discount rate that makes the PV of bond cash flows equal to today's price.
Current yield
Annual coupon divided by current market price. It ignores capital gains and losses.
Holding-period return
Actual return over the period held, including coupon income and price change.
Consol
A perpetual bond that pays coupon interest forever and never repays principal.
Zero-coupon bond
A bond with no periodic coupon; return comes from buying below face value and receiving par at maturity.
01

Debt, Bonds and Trust Deeds

Debt is finance that has to be repaid. Debtholders normally expect interest payments during the life of the borrowing and repayment of capital at maturity. A bond is a tradable form of long-term debt, so investors can usually sell it before maturity rather than waiting for final repayment.

Core definition

A bond is a long-term debt security. The issuer promises coupon payments and repayment of face value at maturity, unless the bond is a zero-coupon bond or a perpetual bond.

The contract is usually set out in a trust deed or bond indenture. Trustees monitor whether the issuer is complying with the bond contract. This is why bond pricing is not just mathematics: the quality of the promise affects the required return.

FeatureMeaningPricing implication
Affirmative covenantRequires the issuer to do something, such as provide accounts or make regular payments.Can reduce uncertainty for lenders.
Negative covenantRestricts issuer actions, such as extra borrowing, asset disposals, or excessive dividends.Can reduce default risk and lower the yield investors demand.
SecurityDebt may be backed by assets that can be used if default occurs.Greater recovery prospects usually reduce required yield.
Debt versus equity angle

Debt can be cheaper than equity, avoids dilution of control, and interest may be tax deductible. The drawback is obligation: interest and principal repayment are fixed commitments, so excessive debt increases financial distress risk.

02

The Bond Pricing Rule

The value of any bond is the present value of its future cash flows. The only thing that changes across bond types is the cash-flow pattern. A consol has coupons forever. A zero has one cash flow at maturity. A coupon bond has coupons plus repayment of face value.

General coupon bond price
[[display:P0 = C/(1+y)^{1} + C/(1+y)^{2} + ... + (C + F)/(1+y)^{n}]]

C = annual coupon, F = face value, y = required return or YTM per period, n = number of periods.

1
Draw the cash-flow line. Write today's price as an outflow and future coupons and face value as inflows.
2
Choose the period. Annual cash flows use annual yield and years. Semi-annual cash flows use half-year yield and half-year periods.
3
Discount each promised payment. Add the PV of coupons and the PV of face value.
4
Interpret the price. Compare price with par to explain premium, discount, or par.
Do not start with the formula as a memory test. Start with the promise: who pays, how much, when, and how risky are those payments?
03

Consols and Zero-Coupon Bonds

Consols and zero-coupon bonds are useful because they isolate the two ends of the bond-pricing logic. A consol has no final principal repayment, so price is the PV of a perpetuity. A zero has no coupons, so price is the PV of the final face value.

Consol and zero formulas
[[display:\frac{Consol price = C}{yZero-coupon price = F / (1+y)^{n}}]]

Worked example: consol

Perpetual Life Corp. issues consols with coupon payments of $60 and nominal value of $1,000. If the required return is 6%, price is $60 / 0.06 = $1,000. If the required return later rises to 10%, price is $60 / 0.10 = $600.

The coupon has not changed. The price changed because investors now require a higher return to hold the same fixed $60 annual cash flow.

Worked example: zero

A zero-coupon bond with face value £100, maturity of 3 years, and YTM of 2% is worth £100 / 1.023 = £94.23. It sells below par because there are no coupons before maturity.

04

Coupon Bond Pricing

A conventional coupon bond, sometimes called a vanilla or straight bond, pays regular coupons and repays face value at maturity. In the lecture example, a 3-year GILT with nominal value £100 and a 10% annual coupon pays £10 in years 1 and 2, then £110 in year 3.

Lecture example cash flows
[[display:P0 = 10/(1.05) + 10/(1.05)^{2} + 110/(1.05)^{3} = £113.62]]

The quoted price is above par because the 10% coupon is higher than the 5% required return implied by the price.

Yield versus couponPrice resultInterpretation
YTM < coupon ratePrice > face valuePremium bond: existing coupon is attractive relative to market yield.
YTM = coupon ratePrice = face valuePar bond: coupon exactly matches required return.
YTM > coupon ratePrice < face valueDiscount bond: coupon is low relative to market yield.

Worked example: 20-year annual coupon bond

A 20-year bond has a 7% annual coupon, face value £100, and annual YTM of 6%. Coupon = £7. Price = PV of a 20-year £7 annuity plus PV of £100 redemption, giving approximately £111.47. Because 7% exceeds the required 6%, the bond sells at a premium.

05

Yield to Maturity

Yield to maturity is the market rate of return for equivalent bonds with the same risk. More precisely, it is the discount rate that equates the present value of the bond's payments with today's market price.

Definition

YTM is the total expected annual return if the bond is held to maturity and all payments are made as promised. It includes coupon income and any capital gain or loss from price moving toward face value by maturity.

In practice, YTM is usually found by trial and error, interpolation, a financial calculator, spreadsheet functions, or solver. The important exam skill is to set up the equation and interpret whether the result should be above or below the coupon rate.

YTM equation
[[display:Market price = PV of coupons + PV of face value, discounted at YTM]]
If a bond sells at a discount, YTM must be above the coupon rate. If it sells at a premium, YTM must be below the coupon rate.
06

Price-Yield Inverse Relationship

Bond prices and required yields move in opposite directions. When required yield rises, fixed future payments are discounted more heavily, so price falls. When required yield falls, those same fixed payments become more valuable, so price rises.

Memory rule

Yields up, prices down. Yields down, prices up. The relationship is inverse and convex rather than a straight line.

The intuition is simple. A bond paying £6.50 each year is less attractive if similar-risk bonds now offer 7.5% than if they offer 3.9%. Investors will only buy it at a lower price when market yields rise.

Seminar-style interpretationHigh value

A 30-year Treasury bond is issued with face value $1,000 and annual interest of $60. If market yields increase shortly after issue, what changes?

Answer

The coupon rate stays at 6% because the coupon is fixed by contract. The price falls because the fixed cash flows are discounted at a higher market yield. YTM rises to match the new market return. Current yield also rises because the same $60 coupon is divided by a lower price.

07

Current Yield and Holding-Period Return

Current yield and holding-period return answer different questions. Current yield measures coupon income relative to today's price. Holding-period return measures the actual return over the time you held the bond, including the price change.

Yield measures
[[display:\frac{Current yield = annual coupon}{current priceHPR = (coupon received + sale price - purchase price) / purchase price}]]

Worked example: current yield

The lecture's 10% coupon GILT is priced at £113.62. Current yield = £10 / £113.62 = 8.80%. This is not the same as YTM because it ignores the capital loss expected when a premium bond is redeemed at £100.

Worked example: one-year HPR

If the investor buys the same bond for £113.62, receives a £10 coupon, and sells next year for £110, HPR = (£10 + £110 - £113.62) / £113.62 = 5.62%.

Common mistake

Do not call current yield the total return. It is only the income yield. Total return also depends on capital gains or losses.

08

Semi-Annual Bonds

Many bonds pay coupons semi-annually. The rule is consistency: halve the annual coupon, halve the quoted annual yield when rates are proportional, and double the number of years to get the number of half-year periods.

Semi-annual pricing
[[display:P0 = (C/2)/(1+y/2)^{1} + ... + (C/2 + F)/(1+y/2)^(2T)]]

T = years to maturity. If the annual coupon rate is 7% on £100, the half-year coupon is £3.50.

Worked example: 10-year semi-annual bond

A 10-year 7% coupon bond has face value £100 and annual YTM of 6%. Use 20 half-year periods, coupon of £3.50 each half-year, and discount rate of 3% per half-year. Price is approximately £107.44. It is above par because the coupon rate is above the required yield.

Worked example: semi-annual YTM

A 10-year bond pays a 10% annual coupon semi-annually, sells for £95, and has par value £100. The equation uses 20 coupons of £5 plus £100 redemption. Solving gives a half-year yield of about 5.42%, so the annual proportional YTM is about 10.83%.

09

Risk, Ratings and Covenants

Bonds are often treated as safer than shares, but they are not risk-free unless the issuer's payments are effectively certain. Investors care about default probability, recovery if default occurs, interest-rate risk, maturity, liquidity, and covenant protection.

Bond typeMain featureExam interpretation
GiltUK government bond, traditionally high quality.Low default risk, but still exposed to interest-rate risk.
Floating-rate bondCoupon resets with a reference rate or index.Less price-sensitive to interest-rate changes than a fixed-rate bond.
Callable bondIssuer can redeem early under specified terms.Call option benefits issuer and can limit investor upside when rates fall.
Green bondFunds projects with environmental objectives.Still priced from cash flows and risk; label alone does not remove credit risk.
Retail or mini-bondMarketed to smaller investors; mini-bonds may be non-tradable.Liquidity and issuer risk need careful attention.
Credit ratings

Credit ratings are agency assessments of bond credit quality. Investment-grade bonds are rated Baa/BBB or above. Junk bonds are below Baa/BBB and therefore require higher yields to compensate investors for greater risk.

What ratings depend on

The two main factors emphasised in Seminar 4 are probability of default and covenant protection. Strong cash flows, low leverage, asset backing, and strict covenants support better ratings and lower required returns.

10

Exam Prompts and Model Answers

Calculation prompt5 marks

If a bond with face value $1,000 and coupon rate 8% is selling for $970, is its YTM more or less than 8%? What about current yield?

Model answer

YTM is greater than 8% because the bond sells below par. If YTM equalled the coupon rate, the bond would sell at par. Current yield is $80 / $970 = 8.25%, so it is also above 8%.

Calculation prompt6 marks

An AT&T bond has 10 years to maturity, $1,000 face value, 8% annual coupon, and sells for $1,100. What are the current yield and YTM direction?

Model answer

Current yield = $80 / $1,100 = 7.27%. Because the bond sells at a premium, its YTM must be below 8%; solving the full pricing equation gives about 6.60%.

Written prompt10 marks

Explain why bond prices and yields are inversely related.

Model answer

A bond's price is the present value of fixed future cash flows. If required yield rises, each coupon and the final face value are discounted at a higher rate, reducing present value. The bond price must fall until the expected return matches the higher market yield. If required yield falls, the same fixed cash flows are discounted less heavily and the price rises. This is why bonds with coupons above the market yield sell at a premium, while bonds with coupons below the market yield sell at a discount.

11

Evaluation and Common Mistakes

Top mistakes

1. Using annual yield with semi-annual cash flows without converting the yield and number of periods.

2. Forgetting to add face value to the final coupon for a conventional coupon bond.

3. Confusing coupon rate with YTM. The coupon rate is fixed by contract; YTM changes with the market price.

4. Treating current yield as total return even when the bond is at a premium or discount.

5. Saying bonds are safe without discussing default risk, interest-rate risk, maturity, and covenant protection.

Evaluation points to use in written answers

Credit risk: promised payments are only valuable if the issuer can make them.

Interest-rate risk: longer-maturity fixed-rate bonds are more exposed to market yield changes.

Liquidity: listed bonds can be sold before maturity, but the resale price may be above or below par.

Contract design: covenants and security can lower required yield by protecting lenders.

One-line memory aid

Price the cash flows, match the period, then interpret premium or discount from coupon versus yield.
