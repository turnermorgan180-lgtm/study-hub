Core finance rule

Use time value of money to compare alternatives that involve different timing, rates, risks, taxes, or repayment structures. A personal finance answer is strongest when it links the product to the household objective.

Key Definitions

Terms to Know Cold

Personal finance
Planning one's own spending, financing and investing to improve financial security over time.
Financial plan
A structured plan covering objectives, budgeting, debt, protection, saving, investing, retirement and review.
Budget
A forward-looking statement of expected income, spending, saving and borrowing needs.
Disposable income
Income available after compulsory deductions such as tax and national insurance.
Liquidity
The ease with which money can be accessed without delay or value loss.
Emergency fund
Cash reserves held for unexpected expenses or income shocks, normally before riskier investing.
APR
Annual percentage rate: the annualised borrowing cost, including interest and relevant charges.
ISA wrapper
A UK tax-efficient savings or investment account where eligible interest, dividends and capital gains are sheltered from tax.
Annuity
A stream of equal cash flows paid at regular intervals, such as monthly pension contributions.
Compound interest
Interest earned on both the original amount and previously accumulated interest.
Part 01

Why Personal Finance Belongs in the Module

Personal finance uses the same logic as corporate finance. Cash flows arrive at different dates, borrowing has a price, saving earns a return, risk must be managed, and taxes can change the final value of a decision.

The lecture starts with a practical warning: individual insolvency is not rare. The slide deck notes that in June 2025, 10,279 individuals entered insolvency in England and Wales, and in the 12 months ending 30 June 2025 the rate was 24.1 per 10,000 adults. That context explains why planning, debt control and liquidity are not optional extras.

Module connection

Bond pricing, annuities, present value, risk and return, and CAPM are not only tools for companies. They also help households compare loans, savings accounts, ISA contributions, pension saving and long-term investment risk.

Part 02

Financial Planning and Budgeting

A financial plan starts with objectives. Short-term objectives might include paying rent, avoiding overdrafts and building a cash buffer. Medium-term objectives might include buying a car, funding study, or saving for a deposit. Long-term objectives include retirement income and protection against major shocks.

1
Set financial objectives. Make goals measurable: amount, deadline and priority.
2
Budget. Forecast income and classify spending as fixed, variable, discretionary or irregular.
3
Manage debt. Identify high-cost borrowing first and check repayment affordability.
4
Protect assets and income. Use insurance or cash buffers where a shock would be damaging.
5
Save, invest and review. Match account type and risk level to the time horizon, then update the plan when income, rates or objectives change.
Budget identity
Net cash flow = disposable income - essential spending - discretionary spending - debt repayments - planned saving

If net cash flow is negative, the household must cut spending, raise income, use savings, or borrow. If it is positive, the surplus can build liquidity, repay expensive debt, or fund investment.

Example: monthly take-home pay is GBP 2,200. Essential costs are GBP 1,150, discretionary spending is GBP 350, minimum debt payments are GBP 180 and planned saving is GBP 250. Net cash flow is GBP 270. That surplus is a margin of safety; without it, a small shock could create borrowing.

Part 03

Borrowing and Debt Management

Borrowing brings spending forward, but the price is interest, fees, repayment pressure and reduced future flexibility. The lecture lists common personal borrowing: overdrafts, student loans, mortgages, credit cards and short-term lenders. Mortgages are usually the largest household debt; credit cards and payday loans are often the most dangerous because rates can be very high.

Debt typeTypical useMain finance issue
MortgageBuying propertyLarge principal, long maturity, affordability depends heavily on rates and income stability.
Student loanFunding higher educationRepayments are income-contingent, so the headline balance is not the same as normal bank debt.
OverdraftShort-term cash-flow gapConvenient but expensive if persistent; may signal poor budgeting.
Credit cardPurchases and short credit periodCan provide more than 30 days of credit, but unpaid balances carry high interest and fees.
Payday loanVery short-term borrowingSmall principal but extremely high annualised cost; lecture slides cite rates around 1000-1500%.
Borrowing cost
Outstanding balance after n periods = initial balance x (1 + period rate)^n - repayments accumulated with interest Debt-service ratio = required debt repayments / disposable income

For affordability, the interest rate is not enough. A loan can have a modest rate but still be unaffordable if repayments are large relative to income.

Credit-card example: a GBP 1,000 balance left unpaid for a year at 20% APR, approximated as 20%/12 monthly, grows to about GBP 1,219 before fees. The calculation is GBP 1,000 x (1 + 0.20/12)12. That illustrates why paying only minimum amounts can become expensive even when the original purchase was small.

Common debt mistake

Do not rank debt only by balance size. A small high-APR balance can be more urgent than a much larger low-rate or income-contingent balance.

Part 04

Student Loans and Repayments

The student loan material is important because it shows that the legal balance, interest rate and actual economic burden are different concepts. Under the lecture's 2025/26 rules, repayments are 9% of earnings above the threshold, interest is linked to RPI, and the debt is written off after 40 years for the newer system. The old system had a higher threshold and a 30-year write-off.

Income-contingent repayment
Annual repayment = 9% x max(0, earnings - repayment threshold) Closing balance = opening balance x (1 + interest rate) - annual repayment

Worked example from the deck: initial student-loan debt is GBP 42,000, starting salary is GBP 30,000, salary grows at 5% per year, the repayment threshold is GBP 25,000, the loan rate is 3%, and payments are annual. In year 1 the repayment is 9% x (30,000 - 25,000) = GBP 450, while interest is GBP 1,260, so the balance initially rises to GBP 42,810.

Using the same annual process, the balance is about GBP 36,952 after 15 years. The balance is repaid during year 25, so by year 26 there is no remaining debt under these assumptions. The turning point happens because salary growth makes repayments rise faster than the interest charge.

Interpretation

For student loans, a higher balance does not automatically mean a higher annual repayment. Repayment depends on income above the threshold. That is why an exam answer should separate balance outstanding, interest accumulation, income-contingent repayments and write-off rules.

Part 05

Savings, ISAs and Liquidity

Saving means giving up current consumption to improve future security. The lecture gives three motives: transaction balances for normal spending, precautionary balances for shocks, and speculative balances to take advantage of opportunities or expected asset-price changes.

Saving optionAdvantageTrade-off
Instant access accountCash available without penaltyUsually lower return.
Regular saverEncourages saving habitMay restrict monthly deposits or withdrawals.
Fixed-rate accountOften pays a better rateWithdrawal before maturity can trigger a penalty.
NS&IGovernment-backed savings productsReturns and access vary by product.
Credit unionMember-owned, often community-basedProduct range may be narrower.
Peer-to-peer lendingPotentially higher returnMuch greater risk and not usually FSCS protected.

For UK savers, tax matters. The lecture explains ISAs as wrappers: eligible interest, dividends and capital gains are not taxed inside the account. Types include cash ISAs, stocks and shares ISAs, Lifetime ISAs, Innovative Finance ISAs and Junior ISAs. The slide deck states the annual ISA allowance as GBP 20,000, frozen until 2031, with separate limits such as GBP 4,000 for Lifetime ISAs and GBP 9,000 for Junior ISAs.

Future value of annual saving
FV of ordinary annuity = C x [((1 + r)^n - 1) / r]

Example: investing GBP 20,000 at the end of each year for 10 years at 4% gives about GBP 240,122. The contribution total is GBP 200,000, so roughly GBP 40,122 is investment growth before considering risk and fees.

Lifetime ISA example: saving GBP 4,000 qualifies for a 25% government bonus, so GBP 5,000 enters the account before investment returns. If GBP 5,000 is contributed at the end of each year for 30 years and grows at 4%, the future value is about GBP 280,425. The restrictions matter: it can normally be used for a first home within the rules or accessed for retirement after age 60.

Part 06

Investing and Retirement

Once liquidity needs and expensive debt are under control, longer-term money can be invested. The lecture distinguishes direct investment in asset classes, such as shares and bonds, from pooled investments, such as funds and ETFs. The correct choice depends on time horizon, risk tolerance, diversification, costs and tax treatment.

Retirement planning asks two connected questions: how much should be set aside each period, and how should that money be invested? Starting early matters because contributions have more periods to compound. Waiting forces a higher monthly contribution for the same target future value.

Retirement contribution formula
Required payment = target FV x r / ((1 + r)^n - 1)

Lecture example: target retirement fund is GBP 500,000 in 30 years, interest is 0.085% per month, and contributions are monthly. With n = 360 and r = 0.00085, the required monthly contribution is about GBP 1,188.

Present-value comparison
PV = FV / (1 + r)^n PV of annuity = C x [1 - (1 + r)^-n] / r

Use present value when comparing a lump sum today with future payments, instalment plans, pension contributions, or loan repayments. Always match the rate to the payment frequency: monthly payments need a monthly rate.

Risk link

Households face investment risk, inflation risk, liquidity risk and longevity risk. A young investor may accept more market volatility for long-run growth; a household close to retirement may prioritise stability, liquidity and inflation protection.

Part 07

Common Mistakes

Avoid these in calculations and written answers

Mixing time units: using an annual rate with monthly payments without converting the rate or number of periods.

Ignoring tax wrappers: comparing gross returns when an ISA or pension wrapper changes the after-tax return.

Treating all debt alike: student loans, credit-card balances, overdrafts and mortgages have different repayment rules and risks.

Forgetting liquidity: putting all savings into fixed-term or risky assets leaves no buffer for emergencies.

Overstating certainty: investment returns are expected returns, not guaranteed returns. Fees, inflation and volatility can change outcomes.

Seminar angle

The spreadsheet practice files are useful here because personal finance becomes clearer when cash-flow comparisons are laid out period by period. For loans, track opening balance, interest, repayment and closing balance. For saving, track contribution, growth and closing value.

Part 08

Exam Practice

Assessment-style prompt10 marks

Explain why personal finance decisions should be evaluated using time value of money, risk and liquidity.

Define personal finance and financial planning. Then explain budgeting, borrowing costs, saving motives, tax-efficient wrappers and retirement compounding. A strong answer does not just list products; it explains why the correct product depends on objective, horizon, risk tolerance and access needs.

Calculation prompt8 marks

An individual saves GBP 250 at the end of every month for 20 years. The monthly return is 0.3%. Estimate the future value and explain one limitation of the calculation.

Use FV = C[((1 + r)^n - 1) / r], with C = 250, r = 0.003 and n = 240. The future value is about GBP 91,830. A limitation is that the monthly return is assumed constant; real investments have volatility, fees and inflation risk.

Debt prompt8 marks

A graduate earns GBP 32,000 and has a student loan threshold of GBP 25,000. Calculate the annual repayment and explain why the outstanding balance may still rise.

Repayment is 9% x (32,000 - 25,000) = GBP 630. The balance may still rise if annual interest added to the loan is greater than the repayment. This is normal for income-contingent loans early in a graduate career.
