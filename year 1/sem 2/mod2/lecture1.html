Introduction to Corporate Finance

Corporate finance begins with one question: how should a firm organise itself, raise capital, invest in real assets, manage agency conflicts, and create long-term value for shareholders without losing sight of risk, ethics, and wider responsibilities?

Key terms

Core Vocabulary

Corporate finance
The study of how firms raise funds, choose investments, manage risk, and distribute value.
Shareholder wealth
The long-term market value of shareholders' claim on the firm, not simply this year's accounting profit.
Capital budgeting
The investment decision: which real assets or projects should the firm acquire?
Financing decision
The choice of how to raise cash, for example through retained earnings, bank borrowing, bonds, or shares.
Capital structure
The mix of debt and equity used to finance the firm.
Real asset
An asset used to produce goods and services, including tangible and intangible operating assets.
Financial asset
A claim on future cash flows generated by real assets, such as a share, bond, IOU, or loan.
Agency problem
A conflict that arises when agents such as managers do not perfectly act for principals such as shareholders.
Agency cost
The direct or indirect cost created by agency conflicts, including monitoring costs and lost opportunities.
Corporate governance
Rules and processes used to direct and monitor a firm, especially management behaviour.
Time value of money
The idea that cash today is worth more than the same nominal cash later because it can earn a return.
Triple bottom line
A broader performance lens using people, profit, and planet rather than profit alone.
01

What Corporate Finance Studies

Corporate finance studies financial decisions made by firms. The lecture frames finance as a practical discipline: managers must decide what assets to buy, how to raise money, how much cash to keep liquid, how much risk to accept, and how those choices affect long-term value.

Definition

Corporate finance is about allocating scarce capital across time under uncertainty so that the firm can fund operations, invest in value-creating projects, and meet investors' required returns.

The module aim is broader than one lecture. It provides foundations in finance, financial markets, corporate finance, and security analysis. Lecture 1 gives the language needed for later valuation topics: if you cannot identify the asset, the cash flow, the risk, and the decision-maker, later formula work becomes mechanical and fragile.

The recurring finance question is: does this decision increase value after allowing for timing, risk, and opportunity cost?

That question explains why finance is not the same as bookkeeping. Accounting records are vital, but finance is forward-looking. It uses expected cash flows and required returns to choose between alternatives before the outcome is known.

02

Organising a Business

The lecture begins with three common business forms: sole proprietor, partnership, and corporation or limited company. These forms matter in finance because they affect liability, lifespan, access to capital, ownership, control, and the scale of projects a firm can realistically fund.

Form Main features Finance implication
Sole proprietor One owner bears the costs and keeps the profits. It is easy and cheap to establish, with relatively little regulation. Simple to run, but unlimited liability and limited life make large-scale fundraising difficult.
Partnership A partnership agreement sets how decisions are made and how profits are shared between partners. Flexible and often inexpensive, but still commonly exposed to unlimited liability and limited life.
Corporation / limited company A separate legal entity owned by shareholders and run day to day by managers. Better access to capital markets and continuity, but more legal machinery, reporting, and governance costs.

Seminar 1 classification logic

Seminar 1 asks which form best suits different businesses. A consulting firm with several senior consultants naturally points toward a partnership or limited liability partnership style because multiple professionals share ownership and management. A house-painting company owned and operated by one student points toward sole proprietorship because it is small, simple, and personally controlled. A manufacturing company with sales of $900 million and 5,000 employees points toward a corporation because it needs scale, continuity, formal ownership, and large capital access.

Common mistake

Do not choose a business form only from the number of owners. In finance, the stronger answer also considers liability, capital requirements, regulation, transferability of ownership, and how long the business needs to survive beyond the founder.

03

Corporations and Limited Companies

The corporate form is central to corporate finance because it separates ownership from day-to-day control. Shareholders own the corporation, but managers normally control operations. This separation creates the need for financial management, governance, and agency theory.

01
Ownership: shareholders, also called stockholders or equity-holders, own the residual claim on the corporation.
02
Control: ultimate control rests with shareholders, but professional managers run day-to-day operations.
03
Risk bearing: many stakeholders bear risk, but shareholders bear residual risk after contractual claims have been met.
04
Legal identity: the company is legally distinct from its owners and must comply with rules such as Companies Act requirements and Companies House registration.
Type Ownership and trading Exam point
Private limited company (Ltd) Shares are privately and closely held, and cannot be freely traded in the public arena. Often used before a firm becomes large enough or willing enough to access public equity markets.
Public limited company (plc) Shares can be sold to the general public and may be listed on a stock exchange. Not every plc is exchange-traded, but plc status brings stricter rules and public-market possibilities.
Evaluation angle

The corporate form is powerful because limited liability, permanency, transferable ownership, and better access to capital markets allow large projects to be financed. The trade-off is cost: legal compliance, tax complexity, disclosure, and governance systems all become more demanding.

04

The Objective of the Firm

The lecture asks: in whose interests is the firm run, and what should the goal be? Possible objectives include increasing market share, reducing employee conflict, survival, maximising profit, and maximising long-term share value. Corporate finance uses long-term shareholder wealth maximisation as the central financial management objective.

Core objective

Financial management aims to make investment and financing decisions that maximise long-term share value.

Profit maximisation is not the same thing. Profit is an accounting measure for a period. Share value is a market-value concept that reflects expected future cash flows, timing, risk, growth prospects, and the information investors have about the firm.

Objective What it captures Limitation
Profit maximisation Accounting earnings over a period. Can ignore cash-flow timing, risk, investment scale, accounting judgement, and long-term prospects.
Market share growth Competitive position and sales reach. Can destroy value if sales are bought through excessive discounts, debt, or unprofitable expansion.
Survival Short-term continuity and avoidance of failure. Too defensive as a full objective; surviving without earning adequate returns is not value creation.
Long-term share value Expected future cash flows adjusted for timing and risk. Must still be reconciled with ethics, sustainability, law, and stakeholder relationships.

Why shareholder wealth is used

The lecture gives several reasons. First, it is practical to focus on a single objective rather than many competing targets. Second, contractual theory says other parties such as employees, lenders, and suppliers have contractual claims, while shareholders receive what remains and therefore need a residual-value objective. Third, in a free-market setting, shareholders supply risk capital. Fourth, shareholders own the firm.

Critical balance

A high-quality answer does not pretend shareholder wealth makes stakeholders irrelevant. It explains the finance benchmark, then evaluates whether long-term share value depends on employees, suppliers, customers, reputation, ethics, and sustainability. The lecture's triple bottom line prompt asks exactly this: people, profit, and planet can support long-term value rather than sit outside it.

05

Investment, Financing and Treasury Decisions

The financial manager links the firm's operations to financial and capital markets. In simple terms, money is raised from markets or investors, invested in real assets, and returns flow back through the firm to claimholders.

01
Investment or capital budgeting decision: what real assets should the firm acquire? This includes factories, pipelines, licenses, technology, or product development projects.
02
Financing decision: how should cash be raised? This includes bank loans, bonds, new shares, retained earnings, and the overall debt-equity mix.
03
Treasury management: how much cash should be held in liquid form, and how should short-term funding needs be managed?
04
Risk and value-based management: how should the firm manage uncertainty while staying aligned with its financial objectives?
Seminar example Classification Reason
Intel spends $500 million to develop a new microprocessor. Capital budgeting The firm is committing resources to a project expected to create future operating cash flows.
Volkswagen raises 350 million euros through a bank loan. Financing The decision is about where cash comes from and how the firm is funded.
ExxonMobil constructs a pipeline. Capital budgeting The pipeline is a real asset used in operations.
Novartis buys a license to sell a drug. Capital budgeting The license is an intangible real asset expected to generate future cash flows.
Pfizer issues new shares to acquire a biotech company. Financing and investment The share issue raises funding, while the acquisition is an investment in assets and future cash flows.
Common mistake

Do not classify by the company name or industry. Ask whether the decision is about using cash to acquire real assets or raising cash from financial markets. Some transactions contain both parts.

06

Real Assets and Financial Assets

The lecture states that real assets are assets used to produce goods and services. They may be tangible, such as machinery and land, or intangible, such as a patent, trademark, customer base, or skilled sales force. Financial assets are claims to income generated by real assets.

Classification rule

If the asset helps produce goods and services, treat it as a real asset. If it is a contractual or ownership claim on cash flows, treat it as a financial asset.

Item Classification Reason
Share of a company Financial asset An equity claim on residual future cash flows.
Personal IOU Financial asset A promise to pay; the holder owns a claim against another party.
Trademark Real asset An intangible asset used to generate sales and protect brand value.
Factory Real asset A tangible productive asset.
Undeveloped land Real asset A physical resource that can be developed or used productively.
Firm's bank balance Financial asset A claim on the bank for money held in the account.
Experienced sales force Real asset Human and organisational capability that helps produce revenue.
Bond Financial asset A debt claim on future coupon and principal payments.
A bank loan is a financial asset for the lender because it is the lender's claim on future repayments. For the borrower, the same loan is a financial liability.
07

Financial Manager Roles

The lecture names the treasurer, controller, and chief financial officer. The distinction matters because seminar questions test whether you can separate finance-market work from accounting-record work.

Role Main responsibility Typical fit
Treasurer Cash management, banking relationships, financing, capital markets, liquidity, and sometimes risk management. Someone trained in finance, financial markets, and institutions.
Controller Accounting systems, internal records, financial reporting, tax information, and performance measurement. Someone trained in accounting and professional reporting standards.
Chief Financial Officer Senior executive responsible for overall financial strategy and coordination of finance functions. Requires both strategic finance judgement and control over reliable information flows.
Seminar-style application 4 marks

Fritz studied finance with emphasis on markets and institutions. Frieda majored in accounting and became a CPA. Who is better suited to be treasurer and who controller?

Answer structure

Fritz is better suited to treasurer because the role is closer to financing, liquidity, and capital markets. Frieda is better suited to controller because the role is closer to accounting records, reporting, tax information, and internal control.

08

Finance Principles

The lecture lists several foundations of finance. These are not throwaway slogans; they explain why later topics use cash-flow valuation, discounting, required returns, market prices, and governance.

01
Cash flow is what matters: value is driven by actual cash received and paid, not just accounting profit.
02
Money has a time value: future benefits and costs must be brought to the present to measure value consistently.
03
Risk requires a reward: investors require higher expected returns for bearing higher risk.
04
Market prices are generally informative: prices aggregate investors' expectations, although they can still be challenged by new information, frictions, or behavioural errors.
05
Ethics matter: ethical lapses are not forgiven easily in finance and can damage reputation, access to capital, and long-term value.

First time-value link

Financial decisions require comparison of cash at different dates. An investor deciding what to pay today for promised future cash flows cannot compare pounds in different years as if they were identical. One pound today is not the same as one pound in 20 years, because today's pound can be invested and because the future pound is exposed to delay and uncertainty.

Quick worked example 3 marks

You deposit £100 at 6% annual interest. What is its value after one year, and what concept explains the second year's interest?

Answer method

After one year, interest is £100 × 0.06 = £6, so the account is worth £106. If the money stays invested for another year, interest is earned on £106 rather than only the original £100. That is compound interest: interest earned on interest.

Common mistake

Do not say time value exists only because of inflation. Inflation can matter, but the lecture's basic logic is broader: money today can earn a return, waiting has an opportunity cost, and future cash flows may be risky.

09

Agency Problems

In large corporations, ownership is dispersed and managers control the firm. Agency problems occur when principals and agents have different objectives. In the basic Type I case, shareholders are principals and managers are agents. The manager may prefer private benefits, job security, prestige, or empire-building even when those choices do not maximise shareholder wealth.

Agency theory

Agency theory describes potential conflicts between principals and agents and proposes ways to reduce those conflicts.

Cost type Meaning Example
Direct agency cost Cost caused by managers extracting value or by shareholders monitoring managers. Managerial perks, excessive pay, monitoring expenses, audit and governance costs.
Indirect agency cost Value lost because the firm misses or rejects opportunities due to agency conflict. A manager avoids a risky positive-value project to protect their job.
Type II / wider agency issue Conflicts between different stakeholder groups or between controlling and minority shareholders. Debtholders, employees, suppliers, governments, and shareholders may want different risk or payout choices.

FineTune plc example

Seminar 1 asks whether directors receiving substantial bonuses and share options despite losses is an agency problem. It can be. If rewards are high when firm performance is poor, managers may be extracting value or insulated from the downside borne by shareholders. The issue is not that all bonuses are wrong; it is that pay should be aligned with long-term value creation and performance.

Evaluation angle

Agency conflicts cannot be eliminated for free. Monitoring, independent committees, audits, and incentive plans all have costs. A good governance system reduces the total damage from conflict, but it must avoid creating incentives for short-term manipulation or excessive risk-taking.

10

Corporate Governance

Corporate governance is the set of rules and processes used to direct and monitor the firm. The lecture links governance to listing requirements, board structure, committees, and published financial statements. Its purpose is to reduce agency problems and improve confidence among investors and other stakeholders.

01
Separate MD/CEO and Chair: reduces the concentration of managerial power and improves board oversight.
02
Independent board members: provide monitoring that is less dependent on management.
03
Remuneration committee: designs executive pay and attempts to align incentives with shareholders.
04
Audit committee: monitors reporting quality, internal controls, and risk management processes.
05
Nomination committee: oversees appointments and succession, reducing the chance of weak or captured boards.
06
Published financial statements: provide external investors with information needed for monitoring and valuation.
Compensation form Likely alignment Reason
Fixed salary Weak Provides income security but little direct link to shareholder value or performance.
Salary linked to profits Moderate Creates performance incentive, but profit can be short-term and affected by accounting judgement.
Salary partly paid in shares Stronger if designed well Managers benefit when shareholders benefit, especially if shares vest over a long period.
Common mistake

Do not write that share-based pay automatically solves agency problems. It can improve alignment, but it may also encourage short-term share-price management or excessive risk unless vesting periods, clawbacks, and oversight are sensible.

11

Seminar and Exam Practice

Lecture 1 is often assessed through explanation and classification rather than heavy calculation. The best answers define the concept, apply it to the specific decision, and then add a limitation or evaluation point where appropriate.

Exam-style prompt 8 marks

Explain why maximising accounting profit may be a poor objective for financial management.

Answer structure

Define profit as an accounting measure over a period, then explain that it can ignore timing, risk, project scale, cash-flow quality, and long-term prospects. Link this to shareholder wealth maximisation, which focuses on expected future cash flows discounted for risk and time. Add evaluation: managers must still consider ethics, sustainability, and stakeholder relationships because these affect long-term value.

Exam-style prompt 10 marks

A plc's directors receive large bonuses after a year of losses. Discuss whether this is an agency problem and how it could be reduced.

Answer structure

Start with agency theory: shareholders are principals and managers are agents. Explain that high rewards despite poor performance may show misalignment or direct agency costs. Then discuss solutions: performance-linked pay, long-term share-based compensation, independent boards, remuneration committees, audit committees, shareholder voting, and the possibility that underperforming managers are replaced. Evaluate by noting that incentives can create new distortions if they reward short-term accounting results.

Short calculation prompt 4 marks

A firm can pay £50,000 every month for 12 months or £600,000 at the end of the year. Which option should it choose?

Answer structure

The options cannot be compared by simply adding the nominal payments, because the timing differs. Use the time value of money: compare both options at the same date using an appropriate monthly or annual discount rate. Without a discount rate, the correct conclusion is conditional, but paying later is usually preferable if there is no penalty and cash has positive time value.

Classification prompt 6 marks

Classify a factory, a bond, a trademark, a company's share, and a skilled sales force as real or financial assets.

Answer structure

Factory, trademark, and skilled sales force are real assets because they help produce goods, services, or revenue. A bond and company share are financial assets because they are claims on future cash flows.

Revision priorities

Prioritise the classification rules, the reason shareholder wealth is preferred to profit, the difference between capital budgeting and financing, the treasurer-controller split, and the agency problem remedies. Those are the highest-yield Lecture 1 seminar links.

Common mistakes checklist

Do not treat profit and cash flow as identical. Do not forget unlimited liability for sole proprietors and many partnerships. Do not call every asset owned by a firm a real asset. Do not say governance eliminates agency costs. Do not compare cash flows at different dates without discounting or compounding them to the same date.
