Key Definitions

Terms to Know Cold

Convertible bond
A bond that pays interest and gives the holder the option to convert into a specified number of ordinary shares.
Conversion ratio
The number of shares received for each bond if conversion takes place.
Conversion price
The effective share price paid through conversion: bond nominal value divided by the conversion ratio.
Conversion value
The value of shares received if converted today: conversion ratio multiplied by current share price.
Conversion premium
The percentage by which the conversion price exceeds the current share price, or by which bond price exceeds conversion value depending on the question wording.
Overdraft
Permission to overdraw a bank account up to a stated limit, with interest charged on the amount used.
Trade credit
Supplier finance created when goods or services are received now but paid for later.
Factoring
Selling or financing trade receivables through a factor in exchange for early cash, less fees and interest.
Bill of exchange
A document promising payment of a specified amount at a future date, often 30, 60, 90, or 180 days.
Hire purchase
Asset finance where instalments are paid and ownership normally transfers after all payments are made.
Leasing
Asset finance where the lessee uses the asset but normally does not become the owner.
APR / EAR
The annualised cost of credit. EAR compounds periodic rates; quoted APR conventions must be read carefully.
Core finance rule

A financing method should be compared using the timing and risk of all cash flows, not only the headline interest rate or the size of the monthly payment.

01

Why Financing Instruments Differ

Debt instruments differ because firms do not all need finance for the same reason. A business with a temporary cash shortage needs flexibility. A business buying a truck needs asset finance. A firm funding a long investment project needs committed capital and protection from sudden withdrawal.

The central trade-off is between cost, flexibility, certainty, risk, and control. Overdraft finance may be flexible but can be withdrawn or repriced. A term loan is less flexible but gives a defined repayment schedule. Leasing avoids asset ownership and can reduce obsolescence risk, but the firm may pay more over time.

Decision factorQuestion to askWhy it matters
MaturityHow long is the money needed?Short-term needs should not normally be financed with expensive long commitments unless certainty is worth paying for.
FlexibilityWill the amount needed vary?Flexible facilities fit uncertain cash needs; fixed loans fit predictable funding needs.
SecurityIs collateral required?Collateral can reduce lender risk and interest cost, but it exposes assets if the firm cannot repay.
ControlDoes the finance affect ownership?Convertible bonds may become equity later, which can dilute existing shareholders.
Cash-flow timingWhen exactly are payments made?Present value, APR and overdraft comparisons depend on timing conventions.
02

Convertible Bonds as Hybrids

A convertible bond begins as debt. It has a nominal value, pays coupon interest, normally has a maturity date, and ranks ahead of ordinary shareholders if the firm fails. The extra feature is that the holder may convert the bond into ordinary shares under specified terms.

Hybrid instrument

A convertible bond is a hybrid because it combines bond-like downside protection with an equity option. Investors receive fixed-income characteristics but can participate in share-price upside if conversion becomes attractive.

From the issuer's perspective, convertibles can make borrowing cheaper because investors accept a lower coupon in return for the conversion option. From the investor's perspective, the instrument is attractive because it offers coupons and repayment if conversion is unattractive, but equity exposure if the share price rises enough.

FeatureDebt elementEquity element
Cash returnCoupon interest is paid while the bond remains outstanding.Shareholder return is possible after conversion through dividends and capital gains.
RiskBondholders rank ahead of ordinary shareholders.Converted investors bear ordinary shareholder risk.
Issuer benefitDebt finance without immediate equity issue.Lower coupon can be possible because the option has value.
Shareholder concernNo immediate dilution.Future conversion increases shares outstanding and can dilute control or EPS.
Evaluation angle

Do not describe convertibles as simply "better debt". They shift risk between issuer and investor. The issuer may save coupon cost, but gives away future upside if the share price rises.

03

Conversion Price, Value and Premium

The lecture slides introduce the conversion ratio and conversion price, while Seminar 5 turns the same idea into calculations. You need to be able to compute the metric and then interpret what it says.

Core formulas

Conversion price = nominal value of bond / number of shares on conversion

Conversion value = conversion ratio × current share price

Conversion premium using share price = (conversion price - current share price) / current share price

Conversion premium using bond price = (bond price - conversion value) / conversion value

Lecture slide example

A bond with a £100 nominal value that can convert into 60 shares has a conversion price of £100 / 60 = £1.67 per share. If the current share price is £1.00, the conversion premium is (£1.67 - £1.00) / £1.00 = 67%.

Seminar 5 example

Pinto plc has £100 nominal bonds, and each bond is convertible into 30 shares. The shares currently trade at £1.50.

1
Conversion price: £100 / 30 = £3.33 per share.
2
Conversion value: 30 × £1.50 = £45.
3
Premium over conversion value: (£100 - £45) / £45 = 122.22%, so conversion is far out of the money at the current share price.
Common mistake

Do not mix the two premium definitions. If the question gives a conversion price and share price, use the share-price version. If it asks how much the bond trades above conversion value, use the bond-price version.

04

Short-Term Bank Finance

Short-term bank finance is used to manage working-capital gaps: inventory must be paid for before sales cash arrives, customers may pay late, or seasonal trading may create temporary borrowing needs.

Short-term bank loan

A short-term bank loan is for a fixed amount and a specified period. It often has covenants, meaning the borrower must satisfy agreed conditions. Its strength is certainty: the firm knows the repayment schedule and the lender normally cannot demand repayment outside the agreement if the borrower complies.

Overdraft

An overdraft is authorisation to overdraw a bank account up to a stated limit. It is flexible because the borrower pays interest only on the amount actually used. This makes it useful for fluctuating cash needs.

InstrumentAdvantagesDisadvantages
OverdraftFlexible; interest only on the amount overdrawn; useful for irregular cash deficits.Can be expensive, may be withdrawn or reduced at short notice, and is unsuitable for permanent funding.
Short-term loanClear payment schedule, defined terms, often lower interest rate, lower withdrawal risk.Less flexible; interest may be paid on funds not immediately needed; may include covenants or collateral.
Seminar calculation logicOverdraft vs loan

Financeme needs £5m for 9 months and £10m for the final 3 months. It can borrow £10m for a year at 8%, depositing excess cash at 6%, or use an overdraft charged at 2% per quarter.

Answer structure

Loan cost: £10m × 8% = £800,000. Deposit income on unused £5m for 9 months at 6% = about £225,000. Net loan cost = £575,000.

Overdraft cost: £5m × 2% × 3 quarters = £300,000, plus £10m × 2% × 1 quarter = £200,000. Total overdraft cost = £500,000, so the overdraft is cheaper and better matched to the timing of the cash need.

05

Trade Credit and Factoring

Trade credit

Trade credit is the simplest form of short-term finance: the firm receives goods or services now and pays the supplier later. It is convenient and often available to firms of all sizes. The hidden cost is that delaying payment may mean giving up a discount or harming supplier relationships.

Trade-credit decision rule

Compare the value of the early-payment discount with the cost of borrowing for the same period.

If the discount saved is greater than the interest cost of paying early, take the discount.

The lecture example uses a £100,000 invoice with a 1% immediate-payment discount. Paying immediately saves £1,000. If the firm needs an overdraft at 6% APR for 40 days, the interest cost is much smaller than £1,000, so paying early is better.

Factoring

Factoring companies purchase or finance trade receivables. They transfer cash to the firm, usually a percentage of the invoice value, and recover the money when customers pay. The factor charges fees and interest on advanced money.

SourceBest used whenMain limitation
Trade creditSupplier terms are available and the firm wants simple short-term finance.Loss of discounts, supplier pressure, and reputational damage if abused.
FactoringCash is tied up in receivables and quicker funding is needed.Fees and interest can be high, and small invoice values may not be accepted.
Bank loanThe amount and repayment period are predictable.Slower to organise and may require collateral.
Evaluation angle

Factoring can improve liquidity without taking a conventional loan, but it is not free cash. The exam-quality answer explains both speed and cost.

06

Bills, Bankers' Acceptances and Certificates of Deposit

The lecture also introduces instruments linked to money markets and trade finance. These are usually short-dated and can support liquidity management.

InstrumentMeaningFinance logic
Bill of exchangeA document promising payment of a specified amount of money at a future date.Can be linked to trade and may be sold in money markets if marketable.
Treasury billA short-term government bill.Generally low default risk and used in money markets.
Commercial billA bill issued by a company.Usually higher risk than a government bill and depends on issuer credit quality.
Bankers' acceptanceA document where a bank accepts responsibility for future payment.The bank's acceptance improves credit quality because the bank, not only the firm, promises payment.
Certificate of depositA time deposit paying principal and interest at maturity.Depositor earns interest but cannot withdraw on demand.

Bills are commonly for 30, 60, 90, or 180 days. The important distinction is who is promising payment and whether the instrument can be sold before maturity.

07

Medium-Term Private Debt

The lecture groups private placements and term loans as private medium- or long-term loans. These are more committed than overdrafts and are more appropriate when a firm needs finance for a defined project or asset base.

InstrumentDefinitionKey advantage
Private placementThe sale of a loan or bond directly to a limited group of investors.Can be negotiated with selected investors rather than publicly issued.
Term loanA direct business loan from a bank for a fixed amount and time, with specified repayment of principal and interest.The lender cannot usually demand repayment outside the schedule if conditions are met.
Syndicated loanA corporate loan made by a group of banks or institutional investors.Allows large borrowing needs to be shared across several lenders.

Collateral may be required for private debt. That can reduce the lender's risk and possibly the interest rate, but it increases the borrower's exposure if cash flows disappoint.

08

Hire Purchase and Leasing

Hire purchase and leasing are asset-finance tools. They are especially relevant when a firm needs to use equipment but does not want, or cannot afford, a large upfront purchase.

Ownership distinction

Under hire purchase, the goods normally become the firm's property once all payments are made. Under leasing, the lessee uses the asset but normally does not become the owner, although some contracts may include purchase options.

FeatureHire purchaseLeasing
OwnershipOwnership normally transfers after final payment.Asset remains owned by the finance house or lessor unless there is a separate purchase option.
Initial outlaySmall initial outlay compared with buying outright.Small initial outlay compared with buying outright.
CertaintyFixed payments make budgeting easier.Fixed lease payments make budgeting easier.
AvailabilityOften available when other borrowing sources are not.Often available when other borrowing sources are not.
Obsolescence riskFirm is more exposed if it ends up owning outdated equipment.Leasing gives added protection where equipment may become obsolete.
Seminar calculation logicLease or buy

A truck can be leased for $8,000 per year paid at year-end for 6 years, or bought for $40,000. It is valueless after 6 years. The interest rate is 7%.

Answer structure

Present value of leasing = $8,000 × annuity factor for 6 years at 7% = about $38,132. Buying costs $40,000 today and has no residual value, so leasing is cheaper on present-value grounds.

09

Valuation Logic and APR

The same valuation logic appears across convertible bonds, leasing, overdraft comparisons and consumer credit: identify the cash flows, place them on a timeline, choose the correct rate, and discount or compound consistently.

1
Define the decision: convert or hold, lease or buy, overdraft or loan, pay now or pay later.
2
List cash flows: coupons, repayments, lease payments, admin fees, discounts, deposits, or interest charges.
3
Match timing: monthly payments use monthly rates; quarterly overdraft charges use quarterly periods; semi-annual bonds use half-year periods.
4
Interpret the result: explain why the cheaper present value or higher conversion value changes the decision.
APR / effective annual rate

If a monthly rate is r, the effective annual rate is (1 + r)12 - 1.

In the Seminar 5 car-credit example, solving the monthly rate from the payment stream gives about 1.818% per month. The effective annual rate is about 24.13%, showing the finance is expensive.

Coursework discipline

The coursework slides stress explaining calculations and typed workings. For this topic, that means showing the equation, stating the rate period, and writing one sentence interpreting the answer rather than leaving a number unexplained.

10

Assessment-Style Prompts

Prompt 1Convertible bonds

Explain what convertible bonds are, their main features, and why they are considered hybrid instruments.

Strong answer checklist

Define the bond element, define the conversion option, explain investor upside and issuer coupon saving, then evaluate dilution and changing risk after conversion.

Prompt 2Calculation

A £100 convertible bond can be converted into 40 shares. The current share price is £1.80. Calculate conversion price, conversion value, and conversion premium.

Method

Conversion price = £100 / 40 = £2.50. Conversion value = 40 × £1.80 = £72. Premium using share price = (£2.50 - £1.80) / £1.80 = 38.9%.

Prompt 3Short-term finance

Compare overdrafts, trade credit, factoring, and short-term loans as sources of finance for a firm with fluctuating working-capital needs.

Strong answer checklist

Discuss flexibility, cost, speed, collateral, certainty, supplier relationships, and whether the finance matches the timing of the cash deficit.

Prompt 4Lease vs buy

A firm can lease equipment through annual payments or buy it outright. Explain how the firm should decide.

Strong answer checklist

Use present value of cash flows, include residual value if any, use the correct discount rate, and evaluate non-financial issues such as obsolescence, ownership, tax relief, maintenance, and flexibility.

11

Evaluation and Common Mistakes

Evaluation points

Cost is not just interest: include fees, lost discounts, compensating deposits, and timing of payments.

Flexibility has value: an overdraft can be worth using even if the quoted rate looks higher, because interest is only charged when funds are drawn.

Ownership matters: hire purchase can suit firms that want ownership; leasing can suit firms worried about asset obsolescence.

Risk is reallocated: convertibles lower immediate cash interest for issuers but create potential dilution and expose investors to equity risk after conversion.

Common mistakes

Mixing rates and periods: do not discount monthly payments with an annual rate unless it has been converted.

Calling the cheapest headline rate the best choice: calculate the cash-flow cost and consider flexibility.

Ignoring conversion interpretation: after calculating conversion value, say whether conversion is attractive at the current share price.

Treating leasing and hire purchase as identical: the ownership and obsolescence-risk differences are central.

Leaving calculations unexplained: write the formula, substitute numbers, and explain the decision in words.
