Core finance rule

For a constant-growth dividend stock: P0 = D1 / (r - g) and r = D1 / P0 + g, provided r > g.

Key Definitions

Terms to Know Cold

Equity
The value of the firm that belongs to shareholders after other claims have been met.
Ordinary share
A unit of ownership in the company, carrying residual cash-flow and control rights.
Par value
The nominal or face value stated on shares; not the same as market value.
Outstanding shares
Shares currently issued and held by investors.
Dividend yield
Dividend received over the share price, usually D1 / P0 for a forward-looking valuation.
Capital gains yield
Percentage return from price appreciation or depreciation over the holding period.
Equity cost of capital
The return investors require on shares with equivalent risk.
Plowback ratio
The fraction of earnings retained in the firm for reinvestment, denoted b.
Payout ratio
The fraction of earnings paid as dividends, equal to 1 - b.
PVGO
Present value of growth opportunities: the part of share value due to future positive-NPV investment.
Part 01

What Equity Represents

Equity is the shareholder-owned part of the firm. Ordinary shares represent this equity claim, so shareholders are the residual owners rather than fixed creditors. They benefit when the company does well, but they also absorb downside once contractual claims have been satisfied.

This is why equity is riskier than debt. A lender expects promised interest and principal. A shareholder has no guaranteed dividend, no promised repayment date, and no fixed maturity value. The upside is that a successful firm can produce rising dividends and a higher share price.

Part 02

Shareholder Rights and Equity Risk

The lecture highlights three central rights: shareholders may have control rights, they may receive dividends if distributed, and they share in liquidation proceeds after higher-priority claims. They can also sell shares in the market, but the price depends on what investors believe the future cash flows are worth.

1
Control: ordinary shareholders can vote on key company matters, usually through general meetings and director appointments.
2
Dividends: dividends are discretionary distributions. A company may retain earnings instead of paying them out.
3
Residual liquidation claim: shareholders receive what is left only after creditors and other senior claimants are paid.
Common mistake

Do not write that shareholders are guaranteed dividends or guaranteed return of capital. Equity value comes from expected future cash flows, but those cash flows are risky and conditional on business performance.

Part 03

Holding-Period Return: Income plus Capital Gain

From a pure financial perspective, investors buy shares for cash flows. Those cash flows arrive through dividends and through resale value. The one-period holding-period return therefore combines the dividend received with the price change over the period.

One-period return

HPR = (D1 + P1 - P0) / P0. Dividend yield is D1 / P0; capital gains yield is (P1 - P0) / P0.

Example from the lecture logic: if a share is bought for 120p, sold after one year for 125p, and pays a 5p dividend, total return is (5 + 125 - 120) / 120 = 8.33 percent. Half the gain is dividend income and half is price appreciation.

Return componentFormulaInterpretation
Dividend yieldD1 / P0Current income from holding the share
Capital gains yield(P1 - P0) / P0Price growth or price loss during the holding period
Total returnDividend yield + capital gains yieldOverall reward for bearing equity risk
Part 04

Share Valuation Starts with Present Value

The basic valuation claim is the same as in bonds: the price of a security is the present value of its future cash flows. For a one-period investor, the relevant cash flows are next year's dividend and next year's resale price.

One-period share price

P0 = (D1 + P1) / (1 + r), where r is the equity cost of capital for investments with equivalent risk.

The discount rate is not arbitrary. It is the expected return available on shares with the same level of risk. If equivalent-risk investments offer 8.5 percent, a share expected to pay £0.44 and sell for £33 in one year is worth (£0.44 + £33) / 1.085 = about £30.82.

Decision rule

Buy only if the market price is below intrinsic value, all else equal. If the same share were offered for £28, the valuation logic says it is priced below the present value of expected future cash flows.

Part 05

The Dividend Discount Model

The dividend discount model extends the one-period logic. The future resale price is itself determined by the next investor's expectations of future dividends and resale price. Repeated substitution leaves the value of the share as the present value of expected future dividends.

DDM logic chain
  • One-period price: P0 = (D1 + P1) / (1 + r).
  • Two-period price substitutes P1 with D2 + P2 discounted at the same risk-adjusted rate.
  • With horizon H, investors with different holding periods should reach the same value if prices are based on future dividends.
  • Unlike bonds, ordinary shares have no specified maturity date and no fixed maturity value.
DDM caseWhen it fitsFormula or method
No growthDividends are constant foreverP0 = D / r
Constant growthDividends grow at stable rate g foreverP0 = D1 / (r - g)
Differential growthGrowth changes over phasesDiscount explicit dividends, then add terminal value
Timing convention

The Gordon formula gives the value one period before the first dividend in the formula. If D10 is the first dividend, D10 / (r - g) is P9, not P10 and not P0.

Part 06

Growth, Payout and Return on Equity

Dividend growth can be estimated from history, but the more useful finance logic asks where growth comes from. If the firm retains earnings and earns a return on those retained funds, future earnings and dividends can grow.

Sustainable growth

g = ROE x b, where b is the plowback ratio. Payout ratio is 1 - b.

This creates a trade-off. Paying out more earnings increases the near-term dividend. Retaining more earnings can increase future growth, but only if the firm can earn attractive returns on reinvested funds.

PolicyImmediate dividendFuture growthValue effect
High payoutHigher nowLower if little is reinvestedGood for mature income shares if growth options are weak
High plowbackLower nowHigher if ROE on reinvestment is strongGood only when retained funds create value
Poor reinvestmentLower nowGrowth may still disappointCan destroy value despite a higher accounting growth rate
Seminar linkQ8

A stock sells for $40. The next dividend is $4. ROE on reinvested funds is 15 percent and the firm reinvests 40 percent of earnings. What is the discount rate?

Growth is 15 percent x 40 percent = 6 percent. Therefore r = D1 / P0 + g = 4 / 40 + 6 percent = 16 percent.

Part 07

PVGO and Cash Cow Logic

A company that pays all earnings as dividends has no retained earnings to fund growth, so a simple no-growth valuation is EPS / r. This is the cash cow benchmark: value comes from current earnings distributed to shareholders.

PVGO measures the part of share price attributable to future growth opportunities. If actual price is above the no-growth value, the difference reflects the market's valuation of investment opportunities. If the difference is negative, investors believe future investments reduce value.

PVGO

PVGO = P0 - EPS / r. Positive PVGO means growth opportunities add value beyond a no-growth earnings stream.

Exam insight

Growth is not automatically good. Retention creates value only when reinvested earnings earn more than investors require for that level of risk.

Part 08

Exam Technique and Common Pitfalls

Most equity questions reward a clear sequence: identify the cash flows, choose the correct dividend model, check timing, discount at the required return, then interpret the result. In written answers, explain that the model is useful because it links price to expected cash flows, but simplified because it assumes forecastable dividends and stable risk.

1
Label D0 and D1. If the question gives the most recent dividend, grow it once before using a forward-looking formula.
2
Check r > g. The constant-growth model breaks if growth is equal to or greater than the discount rate.
3
Separate income and growth. Dividend yield plus capital gains yield equals required return in the constant-growth setting.
4
Use terminal values carefully. In multi-stage questions, discount explicit dividends and the terminal value back to today.
Common mistakes

Do not use D0 in the numerator of P0 = D1 / (r - g) unless g = 0. Do not treat par value as market value. Do not assume a low dividend yield means a low total return; high-growth shares often deliver more of their return through capital gains.

Assessment-style prompt12 marks

Explain how ordinary shares create value for investors and show how the constant-growth dividend discount model links share price, dividend yield and capital gains yield.

Model structure

Define equity as residual ownership, then explain shareholder rights and risk. Set out HPR as dividend yield plus capital gain. Move from one-period valuation to the DDM and state P0 = D1 / (r - g), with r = D1 / P0 + g. Finish by evaluating assumptions: constant growth is plausible for mature dividend-paying firms, weaker for start-ups, no-dividend firms and firms with changing growth phases.
