Equity Valuation and Share Prices

How dividends, earnings, free cash flow, P/E ratios, plowback, ROE, and growth opportunities combine to explain ordinary share prices.

Equity valuation Calculation + explanation MAN00026C
Lecture focus: equity value is the present value of cash flows to investors. Exam answers usually need both the calculation and the interpretation: what part of the price comes from existing assets, what part comes from growth opportunities, and whether reinvestment creates value.

Key Definitions

Terms to Know Cold

Ordinary share
A claim on the residual cash flows of a company after debt and other senior claims have been met.
Market capitalisation
Total market value of equity: current share price multiplied by the number of ordinary shares outstanding.
Dividend yield
Next dividend divided by today’s share price. It measures the income component of expected return.
Capital gains yield
Expected percentage price appreciation. In a constant-growth model, it equals the dividend growth rate.
P/E ratio
Share price divided by earnings per share. It shows how much investors pay for each pound of expected earnings.
Payout ratio
Dividends divided by earnings. It is the proportion of earnings distributed to shareholders.
Plowback ratio
The proportion of earnings retained and reinvested in the firm. It equals 1 minus the payout ratio.
Return on equity (ROE)
Accounting return earned on shareholders’ equity. In this topic it drives sustainable growth when earnings are retained.
PVGO
Present value of growth opportunities: the part of share price created by positive-NPV future investments.
Free cash flow
Cash flow left after operating needs, tax, and investment expenditure; used in discounted cash flow valuation.
01

Share Price Fundamentals

The finance principle is the same as for bonds: today’s value equals the present value of future cash flows. For equity, the difficulty is that cash flows are uncertain, dividends may change, and firms may retain earnings rather than distribute them immediately.

One-period share price
P0 = (D1 + P1) / (1 + r)

D1 is the dividend expected in one year, P1 is the expected sale price in one year, and r is the required return for shares of similar risk.

If the market price is below the present value implied by the required return, the share is underpriced. If the market price is above that value, it is overpriced. The lecture slide example uses a dividend of 10p, sale price of 200p, and required return of 16.67%: the fair price is about 180p, so a market price of 190p would be too high.

Core rule

Equity valuation is not about whether a firm is famous or growing fast. It is about whether expected cash flows, discounted for risk and timing, justify the share price.

02

Dividend Discount Model

The dividend discount model values a share as the present value of all expected future dividends. It is easiest to use when dividends grow at a constant rate forever.

Constant-growth DDM
P0 = D1 / (r - g)
r = D1 / P0 + g

D1 is next year’s dividend, g is the constant dividend growth rate, and r must be greater than g.

The model also separates total expected return into two pieces: dividend yield and capital gains yield. A high-growth company may have a low dividend yield but a high expected capital gain. A no-growth company may have a high dividend yield and no capital gain.

Company typeLikely dividend yieldLikely capital gains yieldReason
Mature cash cowHighLowMost earnings are paid out rather than reinvested.
Stable growerModerateModerateSome earnings are retained at productive returns.
Growth firmLowHighCash is retained for investments expected to increase future earnings.
No-dividend firmZero todayPotentially highValue can still be positive if future cash flows or future dividends are expected.
Evaluation point

The assumptions of perpetual dividends and constant growth are stylised. They are most defensible for mature, dividend-paying firms and weakest for start-ups, distressed firms, and firms changing payout policy.

03

P/E Ratios and Market Interpretation

The P/E ratio compares a firm’s share price with its expected earnings per share. If a company is expected to produce EPS of 10p and the share price is 200p, its P/E is 20. Investors are paying 20 times earnings for the share.

P/E multiple
P/E = Share price / Earnings per share
Equity value = Comparator P/E x Total earnings

A comparator multiple is only useful if the comparator has similar risk, growth prospects, accounting quality, and business model.

A high P/E can indicate strong growth prospects, but it can also happen because current earnings are temporarily depressed. A low P/E can indicate weak prospects, high risk, or simply a temporarily high level of earnings. The lecture’s warning is important: do not interpret P/E mechanically.

No-growth P/E
If g = 0 and all earnings are paid out: P0 = EPS1 / r, so P/E = 1 / r

With a 12% discount rate, no-growth P/E is 1 / 0.12 = 8.33. If the discount rate falls to 10%, P/E rises to 10. Lower risk or lower required return raises valuation multiples.

Common mistake

Do not say “high P/E means good company” without qualification. A high P/E means the market price is high relative to current earnings; the reason could be good growth, low risk, accounting effects, or temporarily depressed earnings.

04

Plowback, ROE, and Growth

For firms to grow internally, earnings have to be retained and invested. The simple sustainable-growth relationship used across the seminars is:

Growth from reinvestment
g = plowback ratio x ROE
plowback ratio = 1 - payout ratio
payout ratio = dividend per share / earnings per share

Retention is not automatically good. It creates value only if the firm earns a return on retained funds above the shareholders’ required return. If ROE is below the cost of equity, more reinvestment can reduce value even though it increases accounting growth.

RelationshipPVGOInterpretationManagerial implication
ROE > rPositiveNew projects earn more than shareholders require.Retention can raise share price.
ROE = rZeroNew projects just earn the fair required return.Retention changes timing, not value.
ROE < rNegativeNew projects earn too little for their risk.Shareholders may prefer payout.
Seminar link

Stormy Weather has ROE equal to the discount rate. Changing plowback from 0% to 40% to 80% changes dividend growth, but price and P/E stay the same because the reinvestment has zero NPV.

05

PVGO and Value Creation

PVGO is the part of the share price attributable to future growth opportunities. The lecture frames equity value as value from existing assets plus the present value of future investments.

Earnings model
P0 = EPS1 / r + PVGO

EPS1 / r is the value per share if the firm pays out all earnings and undertakes no new investments. PVGO adds the value created by future positive-NPV projects.

This is why growth is not the same as value creation. A firm can grow by reinvesting at poor returns, but that destroys value. A firm can also pay high dividends and still be valuable if its current assets generate durable earnings.

PVGO logic chain
  • Start with the no-growth value of existing earnings.
  • Identify the investment cash outflows required for future projects.
  • Estimate the future cash flows or earnings generated by those projects.
  • Discount all incremental project cash flows at the required return.
  • Add positive PVGO to existing-asset value, or subtract negative PVGO if projects earn too little.
06

Multi-Stage Equity Valuation

Many firms do not have one stable growth rate forever. A firm may grow quickly for a few years, then settle into mature growth. Multi-stage valuation handles this by valuing explicit dividends first and then adding a terminal or horizon value.

Two-stage DDM structure
P0 = PV(explicit dividends) + PV(horizon value)
Horizon value at time T = D(T+1) / (r - stable g)
1
Forecast explicit dividends: calculate each dividend during the high-growth or transition period.
2
Find the horizon value: apply the constant-growth formula at the date just before the first stable dividend.
3
Discount everything to today: each dividend and the horizon value must be discounted using the same timing convention.
4
Explain the result: identify whether value mainly comes from near-term distributions or from later growth.
Timing warning

If the first stable dividend is at year 5, the horizon value is usually at year 4. If the problem gives the year-5 dividend directly, do not grow it again before using it in the formula.

07

Free Cash Flow Valuation

Some companies do not pay dividends, especially firms with many growth opportunities. They are not worth zero. Instead, analysts often value free cash flow, which captures cash available after operating and investment needs.

Free cash flow
FCF = Cash flow from operations - Cash flow from investing activities
If interest is included in CFO: FCF = CFO - investing cash flow + net interest paid x (1 - tax rate)

The lecture’s cash-flow example adds back after-tax interest where interest has been included in cash flow from operations, because interest is a cash flow available to capital providers before being paid to debt holders.

DCF value
Company value = PV(forecast FCFs) + PV(horizon value)
Horizon value = FCF(T+1) / (r - g)
When FCF is useful

FCF valuation is especially useful when dividends are zero, unstable, or not representative of the firm’s capacity to generate cash. Dividend models are easier, but FCF is often more informative for growth firms and firms changing payout policy.

08

Worked Examples

Example 1: Constant-growth dividend valuation

Worked calculationDDM

Brilliant plc will pay a dividend of 40p next year. Required return is 10% and dividends grow at 4% forever. Find the share price.

Answer

P0 = D1 / (r - g) = 0.40 / (0.10 - 0.04) = £6.67, or 666.7p.

The dividend is already next year’s dividend, so there is no extra multiplication by 1.04 before applying the model.

Example 2: Growth from ROE and plowback

Worked calculationGrowth

A stock sells for $40. Next dividend is $4. ROE on reinvested funds is 15% and the firm reinvests 40% of earnings. What discount rate is implied?

Answer

Growth = ROE x plowback = 15% x 40% = 6%.

Required return = D1 / P0 + g = 4 / 40 + 6% = 16%.

Example 3: PVGO from a new project

Worked calculationPVGO

Firm Y earns £43m per year forever with no new investments. It can invest £31m today and £33m in one year to generate £36m per year forever from year 2. There are 10m shares and the required return is 10%.

Answer

No-project value = 43 / 0.10 = £430m, so price = £43 per share.

Project value at year 1 = 36 / 0.10 = £360m. Present value today = 360 / 1.10 = £327.27m. Subtract the investment costs: 327.27 - 31 - (33 / 1.10) = £266.27m PVGO.

PVGO per share = 266.27 / 10 = £26.63. Share price with the project = £43 + £26.63 = £69.63.

Example 4: No-growth P/E

Worked calculationP/E

No-Growth Industries pays out all earnings as dividends. Next dividend and EPS are $4. The discount rate is 12%. What is the P/E?

Answer

Price = 4 / 0.12 = $33.33. P/E = 33.33 / 4 = 8.33.

If the discount rate falls to 10%, price becomes $40 and P/E becomes 10. This shows why lower required returns produce higher valuation multiples.

09

Exam Prompts and Answer Structures

Assessment-style prompt8 marks

Explain why a firm can have a high share price even when it pays no dividends today.

Define share value as the present value of future cash flows. Explain that dividends may be delayed because the firm has positive-NPV investments. Link retention to future earnings, future free cash flow, or future dividends. Add the limitation that retention only creates value when project returns exceed the cost of equity.

Assessment-style prompt10 marks

A firm increases its plowback ratio. Explain the likely effect on share price and P/E ratio.

Start with g = plowback x ROE. Then separate three cases: ROE greater than r, equal to r, and less than r. The strongest answer says higher plowback increases growth but does not necessarily increase value.

Calculation prompt12 marks

A company has a period of high dividend growth followed by stable growth. Calculate the share price using a two-stage dividend discount model.

Write down each explicit dividend, calculate the horizon value at the correct date, discount every cash flow to today, and state the timing convention. Most lost marks come from placing the terminal value one year too early or too late.

10

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1: Confusing D0 and D1

The Gordon formula uses next year’s dividend. If the problem gives the most recent dividend, grow it once. If it gives next year’s dividend, use it directly.

Mistake 2: Treating growth as automatically valuable

Growth creates value only when reinvested earnings earn more than the required return. If ROE is below r, growth can reduce value.

Mistake 3: Ignoring the discount rate in P/E comparisons

Different P/E ratios may reflect different risk, not just different growth. A safer firm can deserve a higher P/E even with the same earnings.

Mistake 4: Applying DDM to unsuitable firms without comment

If dividends are zero, irregular, or unrelated to cash generation, explain why FCF valuation may be more appropriate.

Mistake 5: Losing the units

Keep pence, pounds, euros, millions, and per-share figures separate. PVGO for the whole firm must be divided by shares outstanding before adding it to a per-share value.

Final exam habit

After every calculation, add one sentence of interpretation. For example: “The project adds £26.63 per share because its return exceeds the shareholders’ required return.” That sentence often turns a mechanical answer into a finance answer.
