Key Definitions

Terms to Know Cold

Portfolio
A collection of investments held together. The investor cares about the combined risk and return, not each asset in isolation.
Portfolio weight
The proportion of total funds invested in an asset. Weights normally sum to 1, or 100 percent.
Expected return
The probability-weighted average return across possible states of the economy.
Standard deviation
A measure of total volatility around expected return. For a single asset it captures both market and unique risk.
Covariance
A measure of whether two returns tend to move above or below their means together.
Correlation
A standardised covariance between -1 and +1. It tells you the strength and direction of co-movement.
Unsystematic risk
Firm-specific risk, such as a product recall, strike, fraud, or factory fire. It can largely be diversified away.
Systematic risk
Market-wide risk from macroeconomic shocks, rates, inflation, recession, or financial crises. It cannot be diversified away.
Beta
Sensitivity of a security's return to the market portfolio. It measures systematic risk, not total risk.
CAPM
The model linking expected return to beta: risk-free return plus beta times the market risk premium.
Part 01

Portfolio Return

Core rule

The return on a portfolio is the weighted average of the returns on its component assets. This applies state by state and also to expected returns.

Formula box
[[display:R_{p} = w_{1}R_{1} + w_{2}R_{2} + ... + w_{n}R_{n}E(R_{p}) = w_{1}E(R_{1}) + w_{2}E(R_{2}) + ... + w_{n}E(R_{n})]]

Use the same units throughout. If returns are in percentages, keep every return in percentages; if using decimals, convert all of them.

Lecture example: 75 percent auto stock, 25 percent gold

Scenario returns from the lecture slides
StateProbabilityAutoGoldPortfolio return
Recession0.3331%20%0.75(1) + 0.25(20) = 5.75%
Normal0.3333%10%0.75(3) + 0.25(10) = 4.75%
Boom0.3334%-20%0.75(4) + 0.25(-20) = -2.00%
Expected return0.333(5.75 + 4.75 - 2.00) = 2.83%

You can reach the same expected return by weighting the individual expected returns: 0.75(2.67%) + 0.25(3.33%) = 2.83%. This equality is useful because portfolio expected return is linear in the weights.

Part 02

Portfolio Variance and Correlation

Portfolio risk is different. You cannot usually take the weighted average of individual standard deviations because the assets may not move together. The missing piece is covariance, or equivalently correlation.

Two-asset variance
[[display:σ_{p}^{2} = w_{A}^{2}σ_{A}^{2} + w_{B}^{2}σ_{B}^{2} + 2w_{A}w_{B}Cov(R_{A},R_{B})Cov(R_{A},R_{B}) = ρ_{AB}σ_{A}σ_{B}σ_{p} = √σ_{p}^{2}]]

The covariance term decides whether assets reinforce each other's volatility or offset it.

CorrelationMeaningDiversification effect
+1Returns move perfectly together.No risk reduction from combining assets; deviations reinforce each other.
0Returns have no linear relationship.Some risk reduction, because movements are not coordinated.
-1Returns move perfectly in opposite directions.Maximum risk reduction; in special weight combinations risk can be eliminated.
Interpretation

The lecture slide result is the exam point: as long as correlation is below +1, portfolio standard deviation is below the weighted average of component standard deviations. In the auto/gold example, the weighted average of individual risk is about 5.18%, but the actual portfolio risk is about 3.44% because correlation is strongly negative.

Part 03

Diversification and Risk Types

Definition

Diversification is a strategy designed to reduce risk by spreading a portfolio across many investments whose returns do not move exactly together.

Diversification reduces total portfolio volatility because bad news affecting one asset can be partly offset by good or neutral news affecting another. The important limit is that not all risk is asset-specific.

Risk typeAlso calledExamplesDiversifiable?
Unsystematic riskUnique, firm-specific, idiosyncraticFactory fire, bad management, product recall, lawsuit, failed projectYes. Holding many unrelated assets can largely remove it.
Systematic riskMarket, non-diversifiable, macro riskInflation shock, recession, rate rise, energy shock, financial crisisNo. It affects most securities at the same time.

The slides note that an investor holding the market portfolio has only market risk. They also cite the practical rule of thumb that around 30 stocks can be sufficient for strong diversification, although the exact number depends on how correlated those stocks are.

Precise wording

Do not write "diversification removes risk." It removes or reduces unsystematic risk. A well-diversified investor is still exposed to systematic risk, which is exactly the risk CAPM prices.

Part 04

Beta and Market Risk

Finance commonly assumes investors are rational and risk averse. If investors can diversify cheaply, they should not require compensation for bearing firm-specific risk. The risk that matters for expected return is therefore exposure to market-wide movements.

Beta formula
[[display:\frac{β_{i} = Cov(R_{i},R_{m})}{σ_{m}^{2}Equivalently: β_{i} = ρ_{i,m}(σ_{i} / σ_{m})}]]

Beta is the slope in a regression of the security's return on the market return.

BetaNameMeaning
β = 0Risk-free style exposureNo sensitivity to market movements; expected return should be the risk-free rate in CAPM.
0 < β < 1DefensiveMoves in the same direction as the market but less strongly.
β = 1Market riskSame systematic risk as the market portfolio.
β > 1AggressiveMore sensitive than the market; tends to amplify market movements.
β < 0Counter-marketTends to move against the market; uncommon but possible for hedging assets.
Lecture intuition

If a stock has beta 1.5 and the market rises by 1%, the stock is expected to rise by about 1.5% because of its market sensitivity. If the market falls by 1%, it is expected to fall by about 1.5%. This is about average sensitivity, not a guarantee in every period.

Part 05

CAPM and the Security Market Line

Definition

The Capital Asset Pricing Model states that the expected return on a security equals the risk-free rate plus beta multiplied by the market risk premium.

CAPM
[[display:E(R_i)=R_f+\beta_i\left[E(R_m)-R_f\right] ]]

The term [E(Rm) - Rf] is the market risk premium: the extra return investors require for holding the market portfolio instead of the risk-free asset.

Worked CAPM example

Seminar-style calculationCAPM

Risk-free rate = 7%, expected market return = 17%, beta = 1.3. What is the expected return?

Model answer

Market risk premium = 17% - 7% = 10%.

[[math:E(R)=7\%+1.3(10\%)=20\%]].

If another share has expected return 25%, then [[math:25\%=7\%+\beta(10\%)]], so [[math:\beta=\frac{25\%-7\%}{10\%}=1.8]].

The Security Market Line plots expected return against beta. A fairly priced asset lies on the line. An asset above the line offers too much expected return for its beta and is underpriced. An asset below the line offers too little expected return for its beta and is overpriced.

Part 06

Portfolio Beta

Portfolio beta is simpler than portfolio standard deviation because beta is a linear covariance-based measure with the market. You can take the weighted average of asset betas.

Portfolio beta
[[display:β_{p} = w_{1}β_{1} + w_{2}β_{2} + ... + w_{n}β_{n}]]

This does not contradict the portfolio variance rule. Standard deviation depends on covariance between component assets; beta depends on covariance with the market and aggregates linearly.

Worked portfolio beta example

Share A has beta 1.3 and Share B has beta 0.9. A portfolio invests 30% in A and 70% in B.

Calculation
[[display:β_{p} = 0.30(1.3) + 0.70(0.9) = 1.02If R_{f} = 0.5% and market risk premium = 4%, E(R_{p}) = 0.5% + 1.02(4%) = 4.58%]]

Because the portfolio beta is slightly above 1, the portfolio has slightly more systematic risk than the market. It should therefore have a slightly higher expected return than an asset with beta exactly 1 only if the market risk premium is positive.

Beta from correlation and standard deviation

Seminar-style calculationBeta

Security A has standard deviation 9.80%, correlation with the market 0.8, and the market standard deviation is 10%. Security B has standard deviation 12% and correlation with the market 0.2. What are their betas?

Model answer

Beta A = 0.8(9.80 / 10) = 0.784.

Beta B = 0.2(12 / 10) = 0.24.

For a 70% A and 30% B portfolio, beta = 0.70(0.784) + 0.30(0.24) = 0.621.

Part 08

Exam Prompts and Common Mistakes

Assessment-style prompt8 marks

Explain why diversification reduces portfolio risk but does not remove all risk.

Answer structure

Define diversification. Explain correlation below +1 and offsetting returns. Distinguish unsystematic risk from systematic risk. Finish by linking CAPM: only systematic risk is priced because diversified investors can remove unique risk.

Calculation prompt10 marks

Given state probabilities and asset returns, calculate portfolio returns in each state, expected portfolio return, and standard deviation.

Method checklist

First calculate the portfolio return in each state using weights. Then probability-weight those state returns for E(Rp). Then calculate squared deviations from E(Rp), multiply by probabilities, sum for variance, and square root for standard deviation.

CAPM prompt6 marks

A share has beta 1.2, the expected market return is 5%, and the risk-free rate is 0.5%. Calculate the expected return and interpret the beta.

Model answer

[[math:E(R)=0.5\%+1.2(5\%-0.5\%)=5.9\%]]. Beta 1.2 means the share has 20% more systematic risk than the market portfolio, so it requires a higher expected return than the market risk premium alone would imply for beta 1.

Mistake 1 — averaging standard deviations

Expected returns and betas are weighted averages. Standard deviations are not, unless assets are perfectly positively correlated. Always include covariance or compute variance from state returns.

Mistake 2 — mixing decimals and percentages

Do not combine 0.07 with 17 in the same CAPM calculation. Use either 7% and 17%, or 0.07 and 0.17 consistently.

Mistake 3 — calling beta total risk

Beta measures systematic risk only. A company can have high firm-specific volatility but a low beta if that volatility is mostly unrelated to market movements.

Mistake 4 — saying high beta is always bad

High beta means higher exposure to market movements. It increases downside in market falls, but in CAPM it also commands a higher expected return when the market risk premium is positive.

Mistake 5 — misreading the Security Market Line

Above the SML means underpriced for its beta, because expected return is higher than required return. Below the SML means overpriced for its beta, because expected return is too low.

Final exam habit

For every calculation, add one interpretation sentence. For example: "The portfolio standard deviation is lower than the weighted average of individual risks because correlation is below +1." That sentence often separates a numerical answer from a finance answer.
