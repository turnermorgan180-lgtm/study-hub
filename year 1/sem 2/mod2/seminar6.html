Seminar focus

Dividend discount model, growth, payout, return on equity, and share valuation logic.

Question 1

Required return

Full seminar question

Waterworks has a dividend yield of 8%. If its dividend is expected to grow at a constant rate of 5%, what must be the expected rate of return on the company’s stock?

Model answerSeminar

Required return equals dividend yield plus growth. Here that is 8 percent + 5 percent = 13 percent.

Question 2

Implied growth

Full seminar question

Arts and Craft, Inc. will pay a dividend of $5 per share in 1 year. It sells at $50 a share, and firms in the same industry provide an expected rate of return of 14%. What must be the expected growth rate of the company’s dividends?

Model answerSeminar

Using the Gordon growth model, r = D1 / P0 + g. So g = 14 percent - 5 / 50 = 4 percent.

A negative answer would be a warning sign that the formula had been rearranged incorrectly.

Question 3

Brilliant price

Full seminar question

The next year’s dividend of Brilliant plc is 40p per share. Investors in Brilliant require a rate of return of 10%. It is estimated that Brilliant’s dividend will increase by 4% per year forever. What is the share price of Brilliant?

Model answerSeminar

P0 = D1 / (r - g) = 0.40 / (0.10 - 0.04) = 6.67 pounds, or about 666.7 pence.

The key point is that the next dividend is already given, so no extra growth step should be added before applying the model.

Question 4

Finite-growth equity

Full seminar question

Kalvin SA pays dividends that are expected to grow at 7 percent each year. These will stop in year five, at which point the company will pay out all its earnings as dividends. Next year’s dividend is €10 and its EPS at the time will be €15. If the appropriate discount rate on Kalvin shares is 9 percent, what is its share price today?

Model answerSeminar

Discount the first four growing dividends directly. In year five, dividends stop growing and the firm pays out all earnings, so the year-five cash flow is the year-five EPS rather than a Gordon perpetuity.

Using D1 = 10, dividend growth of 7 percent to year four, and year-five payout of [[math:EPS_5=15(1.07)^4]], the share price is about 48.48 euros.

Question 5

DDM assumptions

Full seminar question

The constant growth dividend discount model assumes that dividends are received forever and that the growth rate of dividends is constant. In your opinion are these assumptions reasonable?

Model answerSeminar

They are useful but stylised. The assumption of an infinite life is often acceptable for mature firms, but the idea of one constant growth rate forever is much less realistic.

A stronger answer says the model is most defensible for stable, dividend-paying firms and much weaker for start-ups, firms with changing payout policy, or firms going through distinct growth phases.

Question 6

Delayed dividends

Full seminar question

ABC decided not to pay any dividends to its shareholders for the next 9 years. The company will pay an £8 per share dividend in 10 years and will increase the dividend by 6% per year thereafter. If the required return is 13% what is the current share price?

Model answerSeminar

First find value at year 9 using P9 = D10 / (r - g) = 8 / (0.13 - 0.06) = 114.29.

Then discount that back nine years: [[math:P_0=\frac{114.29}{1.13^9}\approx38.04]].

Question 7

Dividend yield and capital gains

Full seminar question

Consider 4 different equities, all of which have a required return of 15% and a most recent dividend of £4 per share. Equities W, X and Y are expected to maintain constant growth rates in dividends of 10%, 0% and -5% respectively. Z will increase its dividend by 20% for the next 2 years and then maintain a constant 12% growth rate thereafter. What is the dividend yield for each? What is the expected capital gains rate? Discuss the relationship among the various returns.

Model answerSeminar

W: price 88.00, dividend yield 5 percent, capital gains yield 10 percent. X: price 26.67, dividend yield 15 percent, capital gains yield 0 percent.

Y: price 19.00, dividend yield 20 percent, capital gains yield minus 5 percent. Z: price about 171.13, dividend yield about 2.8 percent, capital gains yield about 12.2 percent.

The deeper lesson is that total return can be the same across equities while its composition differs sharply between income and growth.

Question 8

Discount rate with ROE and plowback

Full seminar question

A stock sells for $40. The next dividend will be $4 per share. If the rate of return earned on reinvested funds is 15% and the company reinvests 40% of earnings in the firm, what must be the discount rate assuming constant growth?

Model answerSeminar

Growth is ROE x plowback = 15 percent x 40 percent = 6 percent.

Required return is D1 / P0 + g = 4 / 40 + 6 percent = 16 percent.

Question 9

Two-stock valuation

Full seminar question

Here are the data on 2 stocks, both of which have discount rates of 15%. Stock A has ROE 15%, EPS next period $2, dividends $1. Stock B has ROE 10%, EPS next period $1.5, dividends $1. What are the dividend payout ratios, expected dividend growth rates, and proper stock price for each firm assuming constant growth?

Model answerSeminar

Stock A payout ratio is 1 / 2 = 50 percent, so growth is 15 percent x 50 percent = 7.5 percent. Price is 1 / (0.15 - 0.075) = 13.33.

Stock B payout ratio is 1 / 1.5 = 66.67 percent, so growth is 10 percent x 33.33 percent = 3.33 percent. Price is 1 / (0.15 - 0.0333) = about 8.57.

Stock A is worth more because its reinvestment opportunities are stronger.
