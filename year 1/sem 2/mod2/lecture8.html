Key Definitions

Terms to Know Cold

Cash return
The pounds, pence, or dollars earned from income plus the capital gain or loss over the holding period.
Holding-period return
The cash return divided by the initial investment. It converts performance into a comparable percentage.
Dividend yield
Dividend received over the period divided by the opening share price.
Capital gains yield
Price change over the period divided by the opening price.
Arithmetic average
Simple average of period returns. It is usually the default forecast measure unless the question says otherwise.
Geometric average
Compound annual return over several periods. It shows the exact constant annual return that gets from start value to end value.
Risk premium
Return on a risky asset minus the risk-free return, often proxied by Treasury bill return.
Variance
Average squared deviation from the mean return. It measures dispersion but is in squared units.
Standard deviation
Square root of variance. It measures typical volatility in the same units as returns.
Preference share
A share with priority over ordinary equity, usually a fixed dividend, limited upside, and normally no voting rights.
01

Return and Holding-Period Return

If an investor buys a financial asset, the gain or loss is called a return. Lecture 8 starts with the idea that return usually has two parts: current income and capital gain or loss. For shares, current income is the dividend. For bonds, it is usually the coupon. Capital gain or loss is the change in the asset price.

Core formulas
[[display:Cash return = Income + (ending price - beginning price)]] [[display:\frac{Holding-period return = [Income + (P1 - P0)]}{P0}]] [[display:\frac{Dividend yield = Dividend}{P0}]] [[display:\frac{Capital gains yield = (P1 - P0)}{P0}]]

The denominator is the initial price, because the question is asking what the investor earned relative to the amount originally invested.

Worked example: share return decomposition

You bought a share one year ago for 85p. You have just received a dividend of 10p and the share price is now 90p.

1
Cash return: 10p + (90p - 85p) = 15p.
2
Percentage return: 15p / 85p = 17.65%.
3
Dividend yield: 10p / 85p = 11.76%.
4
Capital gains yield: 5p / 85p = 5.88%.

The dividend yield plus the capital gains yield equals the total holding-period return, allowing for rounding.

Worked example: bid-ask spread

If the bid price is 188.10p and the ask price is 188.20p, the spread is 0.10p. Buying at the ask and immediately selling at the bid gives a return of (188.10 - 188.20) / 188.20 = -0.053% before any wider price movement. This is why trading costs matter even when the quoted market price has barely moved.

02

Arithmetic, Geometric and Blume Forecasts

Historical returns can be summarised in more than one way. The lecture distinction is important: arithmetic average describes a typical single period, while geometric average describes compound growth over the whole period.

MeasureMeaningBest use
Arithmetic averageSimple average of annual returns.Forecasting one future period, unless told otherwise.
Geometric averageConstant compound return that reproduces the full multi-period result.Describing what actually happened to wealth across several years.
Blume estimateA weighted compromise between arithmetic and geometric averages.Forecasting multi-year annual returns when both averages are given.
Average return formulas
[[display:\frac{Arithmetic average = (R1 + R2 + ... + Rn)}{n}]] [[display:Geometric average = [(1 + R1)(1 + R2)...(1 + Rn)]^(1/n) - 1]] [[display:\frac{Blume estimate = [(N - T)}{(N - 1)] x arithmetic + [(T - 1) / (N - 1)] x geometric}]]

N is the number of historical observations and T is the forecast horizon.

Worked example: Blume formula

Over 30 years, an asset has an arithmetic average of 12% and a geometric average of 10%.

For a five-year forecast: [(30 - 5) / 29] x 12% + [(5 - 1) / 29] x 10% = 11.72%.

For a ten-year forecast: [(30 - 10) / 29] x 12% + [(10 - 1) / 29] x 10% = 11.38%.

For a twenty-year forecast: [(30 - 20) / 29] x 12% + [(20 - 1) / 29] x 10% = 10.69%.

The longer the forecast horizon, the more the estimate moves toward the geometric average.

03

Risk Premium

The risk premium is the extra return earned, or required, for holding a risky asset instead of a safer benchmark. The lecture uses Treasury bills as the practical proxy for a risk-free return, while also noting that truly risk-free investments are an idealisation.

Risk premium
[[display:Risk premium = return on risky asset - risk-free return]] [[display:Expected risk premium = E(R risky) - Rf]]

A higher average premium is not automatically a free gain. It normally reflects compensation for bearing greater risk.

Worked example: five-year equity premium

Seminar 8 gives stock-market returns of 31.29%, 23.43%, 23.56%, -10.89%, and -10.97%. Treasury bill returns are 5.26%, 4.86%, 4.68%, 5.89%, and 3.83%.

YearStock returnT-bill returnRisk premium
131.29%5.26%26.03%
223.43%4.86%18.57%
323.56%4.68%18.88%
4-10.89%5.89%-16.78%
5-10.97%3.83%-14.80%

The average risk premium is (26.03 + 18.57 + 18.88 - 16.78 - 14.80) / 5 = 6.38%.

Interpretation

Risk premia are about compensation, not guaranteed reward. A risky asset can have a positive average premium and still have bad individual years. That is why the next step is measuring dispersion around the average.

04

Measuring Past Risk: Variance and Standard Deviation

In this lecture, risk is measured as variability of returns. This is broader than the chance of losing money. A return can be risky because it is far above or far below the average. Standard deviation tells us how much returns typically deviate from their mean.

Risk statistics
[[display:\frac{Variance = sum of squared deviations}{(n - 1) for a sample}]] [[display:Standard deviation = square root of variance]]

The slides use the sample version for historical annual returns, dividing by n - 1.

Worked example: historical standard deviation

Using annual returns of 31.29%, 23.43%, 23.56%, -10.89%, and -10.97%, the arithmetic average is 11.28%.

ReturnDeviation from 11.28%Squared deviation
31.29%20.01400.40
23.43%12.15147.62
23.56%12.28150.80
-10.89%-22.17491.51
-10.97%-22.25495.06

Total squared deviations are 1685.39. Sample variance is 1685.39 / 4 = 421.35. Standard deviation is the square root, 20.53%.

Normal distribution interpretation

If returns are approximately normally distributed, about 68% of outcomes lie within one standard deviation of the mean. With mean 11.28% and standard deviation 20.53%, that interval is about -9.25% to 31.81%.

05

Expected Return and Scenario Risk

Historical statistics use realised past returns. Scenario analysis looks forward. It lists possible future states, assigns probabilities, and calculates the expected return as a probability-weighted average. Risk is then measured by how widely the possible returns are spread around that expected return.

Expected return and risk
[[display:E(R)=\sum_i p_iR_i]] [[display:Var(R)=\sum_i p_i\left(R_i-E(R)\right)^2 ]] [[display:Standard deviation = square root of variance]]
Worked example: Learning Tower of Pita

The share sells today for $80. Three states are equally likely. In a boom, dividend is $5 and price is $195. In a normal economy, dividend is $2 and price is $100. In recession, both are zero.

StateReturn calculationReturn
Boom[$5 + ($195 - $80)] / $80150.00%
Normal[$2 + ($100 - $80)] / $8027.50%
Recession[$0 + ($0 - $80)] / $80-100.00%

Expected return = (150.00% + 27.50% - 100.00%) / 3 = 25.83%.

Standard deviation = 102.07%. The expected return is high, but the downside is severe because the recession payoff is a total loss.

Worked example: stocks versus bonds

With recession probability 0.20, normal probability 0.60, and boom probability 0.20, stock returns are -5%, 15%, and 25%. Bond returns are 14%, 8%, and 4%.

Expected stock return = 0.20(-5%) + 0.60(15%) + 0.20(25%) = 13.00%. Stock standard deviation is about 9.80%.

Expected bond return = 0.20(14%) + 0.60(8%) + 0.20(4%) = 8.40%. Bond standard deviation is about 3.20%.

A risk-averse investor may prefer bonds because they offer lower volatility. A more risk-tolerant investor may prefer stocks because the expected return is higher.

06

Risk Attitudes and Downside Risk

The lecture distinguishes three basic risk attitudes. A risk-averse investor dislikes risk and requires compensation for bearing it. A risk-neutral investor cares only about expected return. A risk-seeking investor is willing to accept extra volatility for its own sake or strongly values upside exposure.

Investor typeDecision ruleExample implication
Risk averseNeeds higher expected return to accept higher risk.May choose bonds over stocks if the extra stock return is not enough.
Risk neutralRanks investments only by expected return.Chooses the highest expected return regardless of dispersion.
Risk seekerActively accepts or prefers more volatile payoffs.May choose a higher-risk share with only a small expected-return advantage.
Downside risk

Variance and standard deviation treat upside and downside deviations symmetrically. In practice, many investors care more about bad outcomes, such as returns below zero or below a target benchmark. Downside standard deviation, or semi-deviation, focuses on volatility below the chosen benchmark.

Exam phrasing

Do not say risk means loss only. The standard lecture measure is dispersion. Then, if relevant, add that downside risk measures are useful because real investors often care more about negative deviations than positive surprises.

07

Preference Shares as Hybrids

Preference shares sit between ordinary shares and debt. They are legally part of shareholders' funds, but economically they have debt-like features: a fixed or pre-set dividend claim and priority over ordinary shareholders. They are not the same as bonds because the dividend can normally be skipped without triggering the same legal default process as unpaid interest.

FeatureDebtPreference sharesOrdinary shares
IncomeFixed interest coupon.Usually fixed dividend.Variable dividend, if declared.
Payment obligationContractual obligation.Directors can normally decide not to pay.No fixed obligation.
PriorityPaid before shareholders.Paid before ordinary shareholders but after debtholders.Lowest priority.
UpsideLimited to promised interest and principal.Usually limited; does not normally share in exceptional growth.High upside if the firm performs well.
Voting rightsNo shareholder voting rights.Usually no voting rights.Usually voting rights.
Core definition

A preference share is a hybrid because it is equity in legal classification but has fixed-income features in its dividend and priority structure.

Firm perspective

Preference shares may be useful when a firm wants to raise equity-like finance without giving ordinary voting control away. The trade-off is that fixed preference dividends can make ordinary shareholders' residual claim more sensitive to weak profits.

08

Preference Share Valuation

The simplest preference share valuation treats the fixed dividend as a level perpetuity. If the dividend is expected to continue forever and the required return is constant, value is the annual preference dividend divided by the required return.

Irredeemable preference share
[[display:\frac{P0 = Dp}{rp}]]

Dp is the fixed annual preference dividend. rp is the required return on preference shares. Use decimals in the calculation.

Worked example: irredeemable preference share

A preference share pays a fixed dividend of £7 per year. Investors require 8.75% on this risk class.

P0 = £7 / 0.0875 = £80.00.

If the required return rises to 10%, the value falls to £7 / 0.10 = £70.00. The price falls because the same fixed dividend is discounted at a higher rate.

Redeemable preference share
[[display:P0 = Dp/(1 + rp)^{1} + Dp/(1 + rp)^{2} + ... + (Dp + redemption value)/(1 + rp)^{n}]]

Redeemable preference shares are valued like a bond-style stream of fixed dividends plus a redemption payment.

Worked example: redeemable preference share

A preference share pays £6 per year and will be redeemed for £100 in three years. The required return is 8%.

[[math:P_0=\frac{6}{1.08}+\frac{6}{1.08^2}+\frac{106}{1.08^3}=94.85]].

This is below £100 because the 6% dividend is lower than the required 8% return.

09

Exam Prompts and Model Structures

Calculation prompt6 marks

A share is bought for 120p, pays a 10p dividend, and is sold one year later for 125p. Calculate cash return, holding-period return, dividend yield, and capital gains yield.

Model structure

Cash return = 10p + 5p = 15p. Holding-period return = 15p / 120p = 12.50%. Dividend yield = 10p / 120p = 8.33%. Capital gains yield = 5p / 120p = 4.17%.

Written prompt8 marks

Explain why the risk premium on bonds is normally expected to be lower than the risk premium on ordinary shares.

Model structure

Define risk premium as return above the risk-free rate. Explain that bonds usually have fixed contractual payments and higher priority in liquidation, while ordinary shareholders receive residual cash flows after other claimants. Equity returns are therefore more sensitive to business performance. Investors normally require a higher expected return for bearing that extra risk.

Preference shares prompt10 marks

Why are preference shares described as a hybrid source of finance? Include valuation logic in your answer.

Model structure

Start by stating that preference shares are legally equity but have debt-like features. They usually pay a fixed dividend, rank ahead of ordinary shares, and usually carry no ordinary voting rights. Unlike debt, dividends can normally be missed without the same default consequences, and preference shareholders rank behind lenders. If irredeemable, value can be modelled as P0 = Dp / rp because the fixed dividend resembles a perpetuity.

Scenario prompt12 marks

Given probabilities and state returns for two assets, calculate expected return and standard deviation. Which investment would you prefer?

Model structure

Calculate E(R) as the probability-weighted average. Calculate each deviation from E(R), square it, multiply by the probability, sum to get variance, and take the square root. The preference answer must depend on risk attitude: risk-averse investors may prefer lower volatility, while risk-tolerant investors may choose higher expected return.

10

Common Mistakes and Grade-Boosting Habits

Common mistakes

Do not divide by the ending price when calculating holding-period return. The return is measured against the initial investment.

Do not confuse cash return with percentage return. A 15p gain means little until it is scaled by the purchase price.

Do not treat arithmetic and geometric averages as interchangeable. Arithmetic is the usual forecasting default; geometric is the compound historical return.

Do not call a preference dividend an interest payment. It is a dividend, even though it is often fixed.

Do not say higher standard deviation is always bad. It is bad for a risk-averse investor unless compensated by enough expected return.

What pushes answers up a grade

State the timing convention and the denominator before calculating. Label whether a return is expected, realised, arithmetic, geometric, or required. After every numerical answer, add one sentence of interpretation: what the number says about investor reward, risk, or valuation.

For preference shares, always explain both sides of the hybrid: fixed-priority features make them debt-like, but discretionary dividends and shareholder classification make them equity-like.

One-line summary

Return measures reward, risk measures dispersion, risk premium links the two, and preference shares are valued by discounting their priority dividend claim.
