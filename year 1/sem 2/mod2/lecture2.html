Key Definitions

Terms to Know Cold

Present value (PV)
The value today of a future cash flow or stream of cash flows, discounted at the required return.
Future value (FV)
The amount a current cash flow will grow to after earning interest over time.
Discount rate
The required rate of return used to translate future cash flows into today’s money.
Compounding
Earning interest on both the original principal and accumulated interest.
Annuity
A finite series of equal payments made at regular intervals, usually at the end of each period.
Annuity due
A finite series of equal payments where the first payment is made immediately, at the start of the first period.
Amortisation
The gradual repayment of a loan through scheduled payments that cover interest first and then reduce principal.
Amortisation table
A schedule showing each period's opening balance, interest charge, payment, principal repaid, and closing balance.
Perpetuity
A constant cash flow paid forever. Its value is finite because distant payments are heavily discounted.
Growing perpetuity
A cash-flow stream paid forever where payments grow at a constant rate lower than the discount rate.
APR / stated rate
A quoted annual rate before allowing fully for intra-year compounding.
Effective annual rate (EAR)
The true annual return once compounding within the year is included.
Core finance rule

Only compare alternatives after expressing every cash flow at the same point in time. A larger nominal payment can be worth less than a smaller payment received sooner.

Part 01

The Time Value Principle

A pound today is worth more than a pound in the future because it can be invested, because waiting has an opportunity cost, and because future receipts normally carry risk. Even when a seminar question says the payoff is guaranteed, the timing still matters because money received earlier can earn interest.

This principle is the bridge between finance theory and decisions. Managers use it to decide whether projects create value. Households use it to compare mortgages, instalment plans, and savings targets. Investors use it to price securities as claims on future cash flows.

Timeline convention

Draw a timeline before choosing a formula. Put today at time 0. Put each cash flow at the date it is actually received or paid. Then decide whether you need to move cash flows left by discounting or right by compounding.

t = 0Today; immediate cash flows
t = 1End of first period
t = 2End of second period
...Regular intervals
t = NFinal date or target value
Exam insight

In written answers, do not say simply that money "changes value". State the mechanism: receiving cash earlier allows investment and avoids waiting, so later cash flows must be discounted at the opportunity cost of capital.

Part 02

Present Value and Discounting

Present value answers the question: what is a future amount worth today? Discounting moves cash flows backwards in time. The further away the payment and the higher the discount rate, the lower the present value.

Single future cash flow
PV = Ct / (1 + r)t

Ct is the cash flow received at time t, r is the discount rate per period, and t is the number of matching periods.

Worked example: lottery choice

The seminar asks you to choose between guaranteed lottery payoffs at a 5% interest rate. The mark-scoring move is not guessing which total is largest; it is converting every option to present value.

Decision

The annuity-due option has the highest present value, so it is the best financial choice under the stated assumptions.

Common mistake

Do not add nominal cash flows across different dates. £110,000 in five years is not directly comparable with £80,000 today until both are valued at the same date.

Part 03

Future Value and Compounding

Future value moves cash in the opposite direction. It asks what today’s investment will become after earning returns. Discounting and compounding are reverse operations: discounting divides by the growth factor; compounding multiplies by it.

Single current cash flow
FV = PV(1 + r)t

The growth factor (1 + r)t captures interest on principal plus interest on accumulated interest.

Worked example: saving to a target

Seminar 2 asks how much must be saved each year to have $500,000 in 40 years at 6% interest. Because the target is a future amount, use the future value of an annuity rather than discounting the target and stopping there.

Future value of ordinary annuity
FV = C[(1 + r)N - 1] / r
Working

500,000 = C[(1.06)40 - 1] / 0.06

C = about $3,230.77 per year.

The economic intuition is that early savings do more work than late savings because they compound for longer. This is why the annual contribution is much smaller than $500,000 / 40.

Part 04

Annuities and Annuities Due

An annuity is a finite series of equal cash flows at regular intervals. Mortgages, car loans, retirement contributions, and some prize payments are all annuity-style problems. The first question is whether payments happen at the end or the start of each period.

Present value of ordinary annuity
PV = C[1 - 1/(1 + r)N] / r

Ordinary annuity means the first payment is one period from now.

Annuity due adjustment
PVdue = PVordinary(1 + r)

Each payment arrives one period earlier, so the stream is worth more. For a loan, that means the required payment is lower.

Worked example: end-of-year versus immediate loan payments

If you borrow $1,000 and repay it in five equal annual payments at 12%, the ordinary annuity payment is about $277.41. If the first payment is made immediately, the stream becomes an annuity due and the payment falls to about $247.69.

Economic intuition

The lender receives money sooner under an annuity due, so each equal instalment can be smaller while still having a present value of $1,000.

Payment-plan comparison

For the store instalment question, assume the product price is 100. The instalment plan costs 25 today plus 25 in each of the next three years. At 5%, its PV is about 93.08. Immediate payment with a 10% discount costs 90, so paying immediately is cheaper in present-value terms.

Part 05

Perpetuities and Growing Cash Flows

A perpetuity lasts forever. This sounds infinite, but its present value can be finite because later and later payments are discounted more heavily. The standard formula assumes the first payment is one period from now.

Constant perpetuity
PV = C / r
Seminar example

A property pays $10,000 per year forever and is worth $125,000. Since PV = C / r, the implied discount rate is r = 10,000 / 125,000 = 8%.

Growing perpetuity
PV = C1 / (r - g)

C1 is next period’s cash flow. The formula only works when r > g.

Worked examples

If the first payment is £150, the discount rate is 4%, and annual growth is 1.5%, PV = 150 / (0.04 - 0.015) = £6,000.

If a project pays £1,000 every two years forever and the annual interest rate is 8%, first convert the rate to a two-year rate: (1.08)2 - 1 = 16.64%. Then PV = 1,000 / 0.1664 = about £6,009.62.

Common mistake

Do not discount a two-year perpetuity using the annual rate. The payment interval and the interest-rate interval must match.

Delayed growing perpetuity

Company X expects first annual sales cash flow of 2,000 units x £200 = £400,000 in year 2, growing at 5% forever with a 10% discount rate. The growing perpetuity value one period before the first cash flow is 400,000 / (0.10 - 0.05) = £8,000,000 at year 1. Discount once to today: 8,000,000 / 1.10 = about £7,272,727.

Part 06

Mortgages and Loan Balances

A repayment mortgage is an annuity: the borrower makes equal periodic payments and those payments cover both interest and principal. The contract rate and payment frequency must match. Monthly mortgage problems use a monthly rate and the number of monthly payments.

Loan payment from present value
Loan amount = C[1 - 1/(1 + r)N] / r

Worked example: house purchase

Mr. and Mrs. Palin buy a £200,000 house with a £50,000 deposit, so they borrow £150,000. The mortgage is paid monthly for 30 years, so N = 360 and r = 0.5% per month.

Working

150,000 = C[1 - 1/(1.005)360] / 0.005

C = about £899.33 per month.

Outstanding balance after payments

The remaining mortgage balance is the present value, at that date, of the remaining payments. For a 30-year £200,000 mortgage at 0.5% per month, the monthly payment is about £1,199.10. After 14 years there are 192 payments left, so the balance is about £147,774.85.

Outstanding balance formula
Balance after k payments = C[1 - 1/(1 + r)N-k] / r

This is the present value, at month k, of the payments still left to make. It is not original principal minus a straight-line share of time passed.

Amortisation tables

An amortisation table shows how each mortgage payment is split between interest and principal repayment. The payment is constant, but the split changes over time. Early payments mostly cover interest because the outstanding balance is still high. Later payments repay more principal because the balance has fallen.

Reconciling the seminar figures

A £200,000 loan gives a payment of about £1,199.10 and a balance after 14 years of about £147,774.85. A £150,000 loan at the same rate and term gives a payment of about £899.33 and a balance after 14 years of about £110,831.13. The method is identical; the difference is the starting loan principal.

Common mistake

Do not estimate the balance by subtracting 14/30 of the principal. Early mortgage payments contain more interest and less principal, so amortisation is not linear. Use either the outstanding-balance formula or an amortisation table.

Part 07

Compounding Frequency and EAR

When interest compounds more than once per year, the effective annual rate is higher than the stated annual proportional rate. The more frequent the compounding, the more often interest earns further interest.

Effective annual rate
EAR = (1 + APR / m)m - 1

m is the number of compounding periods per year.

Question typeCorrect period rateCorrect number of periods
Monthly mortgageMonthly rateYears x 12
Quarterly compoundingAnnual stated rate / 4Years x 4
Two-year perpetuityTwo-year effective rateOne perpetuity period
Seminar examples

14% stated annually with monthly compounding gives EAR = (1 + 0.14/12)12 - 1 = about 14.93%.

To receive £10,000 in six years at an 8% stated rate compounded quarterly: PV = 10,000 / (1 + 0.08/4)24 = about £6,217.21. The EAR is (1.02)4 - 1 = about 8.24%.

Part 08

Excel and Calculator Workflow

Excel is useful for speed, but it does not remove the need to classify the cash-flow pattern. The Week 2 spreadsheet resource is best treated as a check on your finance logic, not a substitute for it.

1
Identify the date of comparison. Usually this is today, but retirement questions may first require a value at the retirement date.
2
Match the period. Monthly cash flows use monthly rates and monthly N. Annual cash flows use annual rates and annual N.
3
Classify the stream. Single cash flow, ordinary annuity, annuity due, perpetuity, growing perpetuity, or uneven cash-flow stream.
4
Check the sign convention. In spreadsheets, money paid out and money received usually need opposite signs.
5
Interpret the result. A lower PV is preferred for a cost; a higher PV is preferred for a payoff or asset value.
Useful Excel functions
=PV(rate, nper, pmt, fv, type)
=FV(rate, nper, pmt, pv, type)
=PMT(rate, nper, pv, fv, type)

Use type = 0 for end-of-period payments and type = 1 for beginning-of-period payments.

Part 09

Exam Prompts and Common Mistakes

Assessment-style prompt10 marks

A firm can receive £90,000 today or £120,000 in four years. Explain how the firm should decide which option is more valuable if its opportunity cost of capital is 7%.

Answer structure: define present value, discount the four-year cash flow, compare both values at time 0, then explain that the opportunity cost of capital is the return sacrificed by waiting.

Calculation prompt12 marks

A household borrows £180,000 over 25 years with monthly payments and a monthly interest rate of 0.45%. Calculate the monthly payment and explain why monthly timing matters.

Answer structure: state N = 25 x 12, r = 0.0045 per month, use the annuity PV equation, solve for C, and note that annual rates or annual periods would misprice the loan.

Checklist of avoidable errors

Using annual N with monthly r; treating an annuity due as an ordinary annuity; forgetting to discount a delayed perpetuity back to today; comparing costs by choosing the largest nominal discount; assuming a mortgage principal falls in a straight line; and writing a numerical answer without explaining the financial decision rule.

Best answer habit

Before calculating, write one sentence naming the cash-flow pattern and the comparison date. That sentence usually prevents the main timing error and makes the answer easier to award marks for.
