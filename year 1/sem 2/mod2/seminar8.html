Question 1

Bid-ask spread

Full seminar question

On the 30th of August the bid price of M&S stock was 188.10p and the ask price was 188.2p. What was the bid-ask spread? Suppose you bought some shares in the morning and later in the day you had to sell them back to the dealer. What is your return on the shares if the bid-ask prices do not change? Assuming this is a daily return, what is the equivalent yearly return?

Model answerSeminar

The bid-ask spread is 188.20p - 188.10p = 0.10p.

If you buy at the ask and sell back immediately at the bid, your return is (188.10 - 188.20) / 188.20 = about minus 0.053 percent. Annualising a daily loss mechanically gives about minus 17.63 percent, which shows how costly repeated trading can be even when prices do not move.

Question 2

Preference as hybrid

Full seminar question

Why are preference shares a hybrid between equity and shares?

Model answerSeminar

Preference shares are hybrid because legally they are equity, but economically they contain debt-like features such as fixed or pre-set dividend claims and priority over ordinary shareholders.

They sit between bonds and common shares in both risk and rights.

Question 3

Risk premium series

Full seminar question

Here are the stock market and Treasury bill returns over five years. What was the risk premium on shares each year? What was the average risk premium? What was the annual stock market geometric return over the 5 years?

Model answerSeminar

Yearly risk premia are 26.03, 18.57, 18.88, minus 16.78, and minus 14.80 percent. The average risk premium is about 6.38 percent.

The annual geometric stock-market return over the five years is about 9.70 percent, which is lower than the arithmetic average because volatility drags down compound growth.

Question 4

Share return decomposition

Full seminar question

You bought a share 1 year ago for 85p; you have just received a dividend of 10p and the share’s market price is today 90p. What is your cash return, percentage return, dividend yield, and capital gains yield?

Model answerSeminar

Cash return is dividend plus price change = 10 + (90 - 85) = 15p.

Percentage return is 15 / 85 = 17.65 percent. Dividend yield is 10 / 85 = 11.76 percent and capital gains yield is 5 / 85 = 5.88 percent.

Question 5

Bond return decomposition

Full seminar question

Suppose you bought a 5% annual coupon bond 1 year ago with nominal value £100. The bond was bought at £104 and can now be sold at £103. What was your cash return and percentage return? What is this percentage return called? Recall the yield to maturity of bonds. What is the difference between yield to maturity and percentage return?

Model answerSeminar

Cash return is 5 + (103 - 104) = 4.

Percentage holding-period return is 4 / 104 = 3.85 percent. This is a realised holding-period return, not the same thing as YTM, which assumes holding to maturity and discounts all remaining promised cash flows.

Question 6

Bond versus equity premia

Full seminar question

Explain why we should expect the risk premium of bonds to be smaller than the risk premium of shares.

Model answerSeminar

Bonds normally have prior claims on cash flow, lower payoff volatility, and contractually fixed payments, so their cash flows are typically less sensitive to business conditions than equity cash flows.

Because investors bear less systematic risk, the required premium is usually lower.

Question 7

Blume formula

Full seminar question

Over a 30-year period an asset had an arithmetic average of 12% and a geometric return of 10%. Using Blume’s formula, what is your best estimate of the future annual returns over 5 years, 10 years, and 20 years?

Model answerSeminar

Using 30 years of data with arithmetic mean 12 percent and geometric mean 10 percent, the five-year estimate is about 11.72 percent.

The ten-year estimate is about 11.38 percent and the twenty-year estimate about 10.69 percent. As the horizon lengthens, the estimate moves closer to the geometric mean.

Question 10

Scenario analysis for one stock

Full seminar question

Considering 3 different scenarios of the economy, the share of Learning Tower of Pita, Inc. is expected to generate the following payoffs next year: boom dividend $5 and stock price $195; normal economy dividend $2 and stock price $100; recession 0 and 0. The stock sells today for $80 and the three states are equally likely. Calculate the expected return and standard deviation.

Model answerSeminar

State returns are 150 percent in a boom, 27.5 percent in a normal economy, and minus 100 percent in a recession, using current price 80 and including dividends.

With equal probabilities, expected return is about 25.83 percent and standard deviation is about 102.07 percent, which tells you the stock is extremely risky despite a high expected return.

Question 11

Stocks versus bonds scenarios

Full seminar question

Consider the following scenario analysis: recession probability 0.20, stock return -5%, bond return 14%; normal economy probability 0.60, stock return 15%, bond return 8%; boom probability 0.20, stock return 25%, bond return 4%. Is it reasonable to assume Treasury Bills will provide higher returns in recessions than in booms? Calculate the expected return and standard deviation for each investment. Which would you prefer?

Model answerSeminar

Expected return is 13 percent for stocks and 8.4 percent for bonds. Standard deviation is about 9.80 percent for stocks and 3.20 percent for bonds.

Which one is preferred depends on risk appetite. A risk-averse investor usually chooses bonds here; a risk-tolerant investor may prefer stocks for the higher expected return.

Question 12

Risk-taking investor choice

Full seminar question

Consider the returns of XV plc and ZD plc under equal-probability states of the economy: recession 3% and 20%; normal 8% and 12%; boom 16% and -2%. Which of these individual shares is preferred by a risk-taking investor?

Model answerSeminar

XV has expected return of 9 percent with standard deviation about 5.35 percent. ZD has expected return of 10 percent with standard deviation about 9.09 percent.

A risk-taking investor would likely prefer ZD because it offers the higher expected return and greater upside variation.

Question 13

Normal-distribution interval

Full seminar question

Using the results from question 12 and assuming returns are normally distributed, how confident in probability terms are you that return of ZD will be between 37.27% and -17.27%?

Model answerSeminar

For ZD, the interval 37.27 percent to minus 17.27 percent is essentially the mean plus or minus two standard deviations.

Under the normal distribution approximation, about 95 percent of outcomes lie within that range.

Question 14

Risk aversion reflection

Full seminar question

Estimate your risk aversion using the slides at the end of last week’s lecture. Which type of investor are you?

Model answerSeminar

A strong answer classifies you by the trade-off you accept between expected return and volatility, not by personality language alone.

For example, a cautious investor values downside protection first, a balanced investor accepts moderate volatility for growth, and an aggressive investor prioritises expected return and tolerates large short-run swings.

Question 15

Excel workflow

Full seminar question

Download the excel file “Price History”. Estimate in Excel the daily return and the standard deviation for each company using Excel formulas. Estimate also the annual return and annual standard deviation.

Model answerSeminar

Compute daily returns as (Pt / Pt-1) - 1, then use AVERAGE for mean daily return and STDEV.S for daily standard deviation.

To annualise, multiply mean daily return by the number of trading days and multiply daily standard deviation by the square root of trading days. The key is to annualise volatility differently from mean return.
