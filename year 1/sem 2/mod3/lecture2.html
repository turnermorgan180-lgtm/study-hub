Core quantitative rule

Probability is the language of uncertainty. Expected value is the benchmark for decisions under uncertainty, but conditional probability often changes how evidence should be read.

Part 01

Probability as a business tool

Probability is not only about games of chance. It supports business decisions whenever outcomes are uncertain: customer churn, product defect rates, risk events, screening tests, supplier failure, portfolio performance, or demand scenarios.

The exam-relevant habit is to define the event clearly before calculating. If the event is vague, the probability expression usually goes wrong.

Part 02

Basic and conditional probability

Unconditional probability measures how likely an event is overall. Conditional probability measures how likely one event is given that another has already happened. This distinction is essential in contingency tables and diagnostic testing questions.

The mark-scoring step is to get the denominator right. In a conditional probability, the condition defines the relevant comparison group. If the condition is blue, your denominator must come from the blue cases, not the whole sample.

Part 03

Expected value and decision logic

Expected value is the weighted average outcome across possible states. It does not tell you what will happen in any one case, but it does provide the correct long-run benchmark for comparing uncertain options.

In applied questions, you should state that expected value combines payoff size and probability. A very large payoff with tiny probability may contribute less than a moderate payoff with a much larger probability.

Part 04

Bayes-style reasoning in testing

Diagnostic questions often trap students because sensitivity and specificity are about test performance conditional on the true state, while the manager usually wants the reverse: what is the chance the state is true given the observed test result.

That reversal is why a positive test result can still imply a modest probability of actually having the condition when prevalence is low. The stronger answer explains this rather than just quoting the final number.

Part 05

Combinations and permutations

Counting methods matter whenever you need to work out how many arrangements are possible. If order does not matter, combinations are appropriate. If roles are distinct, order matters and permutations should be used instead.

The exam benefit of saying this in words is that it shows the marker you understand the structure of the problem, not just the formula.

Part 06

How to write this in the exam

State the event, define the denominator, perform the calculation, and then interpret it in words. For expected-value questions, finish with a decision sentence. For conditional-probability questions, finish by explaining what the condition means in context.

Seminar angle

Week 2 is one of the most directly examinable weeks because expected payoffs, conditional probability, and diagnostic testing all translate into short, high-mark questions.

Common mistake

The classic error is to confuse P(A|B) with P(B|A). In testing questions this flips the managerial interpretation completely.

Assessment-style prompt10 marks

A screening test has known sensitivity and specificity. Explain why a positive result should not automatically be interpreted as a high probability that the condition is present.

Do not stop at calculation. Show the correct method, explain the logic, and then state what the result means in a business or research context.
