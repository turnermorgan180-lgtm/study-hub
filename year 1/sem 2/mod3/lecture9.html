Core quantitative rule

Software does not choose the method for you. SPSS speeds up analysis, but the marks still come from selecting the right technique and interpreting the output correctly.

Applied Workflow
Practical workflow
Inspect variables: nominal, ordinal, or scale.
Graph first: use charts to understand shape and grouping.
Summarise next: descriptives, crosstabs, or explore output.
Then test: only after the structure of the data is clear.
Interpret: explain what the output means for the research question.
Part 01

What SPSS is for

SPSS provides a workflow for graphing, summarising, cross-tabulating, exploring, and testing data efficiently. That is valuable because business analysis often requires repeated comparisons, grouped summaries, and clear exportable output rather than one-off manual calculations.

But the software is only as good as the decisions made before clicking OK. If the method is wrong, the output may be tidy but still meaningless.

Part 02

Choosing the right graph and summary

The practical materials make clear that graph type depends on variable type. Histograms, boxplots, and scatterplots suit scale variables. Bar charts and crosstabs suit nominal or ordinal categories. Grouped versions help reveal structure that would otherwise be hidden.

This is not only a software skill. It is an applied-statistics judgement about how evidence should be represented.

Part 03

Exploration before formal inference

A strong quantitative workflow does not jump straight to a test. It starts by looking at distributions, outliers, grouped patterns, and possible confounders. SPSS makes that exploration easier through boxplots, grouped scatterplots, Explore output, and crosstabs.

That matters because the data structure often tells you which formal method is appropriate later.

Part 04

Controlling for a third variable

The practical worksheet explicitly introduces the idea that an apparent relationship can shift when a previously ignored variable is brought into the analysis. This is one of the most important lessons of applied statistics.

A better answer therefore does not stop at a two-variable relationship if a third variable may be reshaping it.

Part 05

Turning output into argument

Graphs, tables, and SPSS output are not self-explanatory. The analyst has to say what the result shows, whether it is large or small, whether the comparison is meaningful, and what caution is required.

In coursework especially, explanation and structure matter as much as technical correctness.

Part 06

How to write this in the exam or coursework

Identify the variable types, justify the graph or test, present the main result, and then interpret it in plain language. If software output appears in the answer, it should support the argument rather than replace it.

Common mistake

A weak practical answer pastes SPSS output without saying why that graph or table was chosen or what it implies. Output is evidence, not the argument itself.

Assessment-style prompt10 marks

Explain why quantitative software should be used as part of a structured analysis process rather than as a substitute for statistical reasoning.

Do not stop at calculation. Show the correct method, explain the logic, and then state what the result means in a business or research context.
