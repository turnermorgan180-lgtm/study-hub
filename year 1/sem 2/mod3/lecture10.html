Core quantitative rule

Choose the test from the variables first, then read the p-value. If Sig. is below 0.05, reject the null at the 5 percent level; if it is above 0.05, fail to reject the null.

Test Selection Map
One continuous variable versus a fixed value: One-Sample T Test.
Two continuous measurements on the same people: Paired-Samples T Test.
One continuous variable across two groups: Independent-Samples T Test.
One continuous variable across three or more groups: One-Way ANOVA.
One categorical variable against expected frequencies: chi-squared goodness of fit, or binomial test when there are only two categories.
Two categorical variables together: chi-squared test of association using Crosstabs.
Part 01

The Week 10 Decision Problem

Week 10 is about using SPSS for hypothesis testing rather than only description. The worksheet starts from the same class dataset used in the earlier SPSS practical, so the variables are familiar: height, weight, guessed class height, guessed class weight, gender, breakfast, college, and confidence with numbers.

The practical point is that the dataset contains both continuous variables and categorical variables. That mixture is exactly why method choice matters. A mean-based t-test is sensible for height and weight, but not for college or gender. A chi-squared test is natural for categories, but it does not compare means. SPSS will often let you run an unsuitable procedure, so the analyst has to think before trusting the output.

Part 02

Reading Sig. as the P-Value

The Week 10 materials emphasise that the SPSS Sig. value is the p-value. This is the number used to decide whether the observed difference or association is statistically significant at the chosen confidence level.

Decision routine
1
State the null.

Usually this says there is no difference, no association, or that observed frequencies match expected frequencies.

2
Read Sig.

Use the relevant Sig. entry from the correct row of SPSS output.

3
Compare with 0.05.

Below 0.05 means statistically significant at the 5 percent level; above 0.05 means not statistically significant at that level.

4
Conclude in context.

Say what that means for height, weight, college, breakfast, gender, or whichever variables the question actually used.

Part 03

T-Tests in Compare Means

SPSS groups the main t-test procedures under Analyze - Compare Means. The Week 10 worksheet uses these options to show the differences between one-sample, paired-sample, and independent-sample logic.

T-test comparison
ProcedureQuestion it answersWeek 10 example
One-Sample T TestIs one sample mean significantly different from a fixed test value?Compare actual Height with the mean GuessHeight value of 1.7207, and actual Weight with the mean GuessWeight value of 68.57.
Paired-Samples T TestAre two continuous measurements from the same people significantly different?Compare Height with GuessHeight, then Weight with GuessWeight, for the same respondents.
Independent-Samples T TestDo two separate groups have significantly different means?Compare Height by Gender, defining the groups as 0 and 1; then compare Weight by Breakfast.

The solutions show why interpretation matters. The average class guess for height was 1.457cm above the actual mean, but this was not statistically significant because p = 0.150. The average class guess for weight was 7.061kg too high, and this was statistically significant because p = 0.000.

For the independent-samples test, SPSS reports Levene's test for equality of variances before the t-test rows. In the worksheet solutions, the variance checks are not significant, so the equal-variances-assumed row is used. The Gender and Height comparison is significant, while the Breakfast and Weight comparison is also significant, partly because the breakfast grouping overlaps with gender composition in the dataset.

Part 04

ANOVA for Grouped Means

One-Way ANOVA also sits under Analyze - Compare Means. It extends the mean-comparison logic from two groups to two or more groups. The null hypothesis is that all group means are equal. The alternative is not that every group differs from every other group; it is simply that the means are not all the same.

In the Week 10 task, College is used as the categorical factor and Height or Weight is used as the continuous dependent variable. This asks whether any college has a significantly different mean height or mean weight from the others.

ANOVA interpretation

If ANOVA is significant, at least one group mean differs. ANOVA alone does not tell you exactly which groups differ unless you run appropriate follow-up comparisons.

The solutions report p = 0.406 for Height by College and p = 0.351 for Weight by College. Both are above 0.05, so the correct conclusion is that there is no statistically significant evidence that college groups differ in mean height or mean weight in this dataset.

Part 05

Chi-Squared Tests in SPSS

The Week 10 worksheet separates chi-squared logic into two forms. A one-dimensional test compares one categorical variable with expected frequencies. A two-dimensional test of association compares the observed cross-tabulation of two categorical variables with the cross-tabulation expected if the variables were independent.

Chi-squared comparison
TestVariable structureNull hypothesis
Goodness of fitOne categorical variableThe observed category frequencies match the expected category frequencies.
Test of associationTwo categorical variablesThe two categorical variables are independent.

For Gender and Breakfast, SPSS uses a binomial-style one-sample nonparametric test because each variable has two categories. The solutions state that there are significantly different numbers of men and women, and significantly different numbers of people who did and did not have breakfast.

For College, the goodness-of-fit test rejects equal category frequencies. This is unsurprising because colleges do not all have the same number of people in the dataset.

For ConfidentWithNumbers by Gender, the worksheet uses Analyze - Descriptive Statistics - Crosstabs, adds Chi-square in Statistics, and adds expected counts in Cells. The largest count-versus-expected difference is in the Disagree row, most pronounced in the Male column. The Pearson chi-squared p-value is 0.322, so the correct conclusion is that there is no statistically significant association between gender and confidence with numbers in this sample.

Part 06

Normality and Reliability Checks

The worksheet closes by reminding you that t-tests and ANOVA work best when the relevant continuous data are approximately normally distributed. SPSS can check continuous variables against a normal distribution through Analyze - Nonparametric Tests - One Sample.

The Week 10 solution states that only Weight appears not to be normally distributed. That matters because a non-normal distribution can make mean-based inference less reliable, especially in small samples or when outliers are influential.

Common mistake

Do not treat every statistically significant output as equally reliable. Check whether the test assumptions are plausible and whether warning signs appear, such as expected chi-squared counts below 5.

Part 07

How to Write This Up

A strong Week 10 answer follows a consistent structure: identify the variable types, choose the test, state the null hypothesis, report the relevant Sig. value, compare it with 0.05, and write a contextual conclusion.

Exam and coursework angle

The marks are not just for finding the menu item. They come from showing why the method matches the data and explaining what the SPSS result means in ordinary business language.

Assessment-style prompt10 marks

A researcher wants to test whether a categorical grouping variable is related to a continuous outcome variable. Explain which SPSS procedure should be used, how the null hypothesis should be framed, and how the Sig. value should be interpreted.

A high-mark answer identifies ANOVA or an appropriate t-test depending on the number of groups, states that the null is no difference in group means, reports whether p is below 0.05, and finishes with a conclusion tied to the real variables.
