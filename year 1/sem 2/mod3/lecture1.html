Core quantitative rule

Describe the data first, but describe it intelligently. The right summary depends on data type, shape, and the presence of outliers.

Decision Map
Visual guide
Continuous data: use histogram, boxplot, mean, median, IQR, standard deviation.
Categorical data: use bar chart, frequencies, proportions, crosstabs.
Outliers present: trust median and IQR more than mean and range.
Need comparability: standardise using z-scores.
Part 01

Why descriptive statistics matter

Descriptive statistics turn raw observations into a form that a manager can actually use. They answer first-order questions such as what a typical value looks like, how dispersed the data are, whether the distribution is symmetric or skewed, and whether any observations look unusual enough to investigate further.

That matters because business data are rarely neutral. A single outlier may signal a recording error, a fraud case, an exceptional customer, or a one-off shock. The stronger answer does not just report a statistic. It explains what that statistic says about the dataset and whether it is trustworthy as a summary of the whole sample.

Part 02

Choosing a measure of centre

The mean uses all observations and is therefore informative when the data are roughly balanced and free from severe distortion. The median instead focuses on the middle position and is usually safer when outliers or skew pull the mean away from what looks typical.

In exam terms, the scoring move is to justify the choice. If the distribution is fairly symmetric, the mean can be defended. If there are extreme values or a long tail, the median is usually the stronger summary. Saying that explicitly is better than reporting both and moving on.

Part 03

Choosing a measure of spread

Range, inter-quartile range, and standard deviation capture different things. The range uses only the extreme values and is therefore very sensitive to unusual observations. The inter-quartile range focuses on the middle fifty percent and is much more robust. Standard deviation measures typical distance from the mean and becomes especially useful later when we standardise observations or work with distributions.

A common exam trap is to forget that spread has to be interpreted relative to centre. A standard deviation of 10 means something very different when the mean is 12 than when the mean is 1,200. That is why the coefficient of variation becomes useful in relative-comparison questions.

Part 04

Charts as diagnostic tools

A chart is not decoration. It is a diagnostic device. Histograms and boxplots are useful for continuous data. Bar charts and crosstabs suit categorical data. Scatterplots suit pairs of continuous variables. If the graph type does not match the data type, the output may still exist, but it will not mean anything useful.

Boxplots are especially valuable because they combine centre, spread, and outlier detection in one compact visual. They are often the fastest way to discuss skew and unusually distant cases in a business dataset.

Part 05

Standardisation and comparability

A z-score expresses how far an observation sits from the mean in standard-deviation units. That makes it possible to compare values from different scales in a disciplined way. A value with [[math:z=2]] is two standard deviations above the mean regardless of whether the original variable was revenue, waiting time, or weight.

This is a bridge topic. Descriptive statistics here feed directly into the normal-distribution, confidence-interval, and testing material later in the module.

Part 06

How to write this in the exam

Start by naming the appropriate summary or graph. Then explain why it is appropriate for the shape and type of the data. Then interpret what it shows. If an outlier is present, do not ignore it. If skew is visible, say how that affects which summary you trust most.

Weak answers stop at computation. Strong answers translate the statistic into a statement about the business data.

Seminar angle

Week 1 makes you compute medians from frequency tables, five-number summaries, coefficients of variation, and z-scores. Those are not isolated drills. They are the basic language of the entire module.

Common mistake

A common mistake is to treat every summary as equally valid for every dataset. If the data are skewed, saying “the mean is 54” without qualification can be weaker than saying “the median is more representative because the distribution is right-skewed.”

Assessment-style prompt10 marks

A retailer has weekly sales data with a few extremely high weeks caused by promotions. Explain which measures of centre and spread you would report, and justify your choice.

Do not stop at calculation. Show the correct method, explain the logic, and then state what the result means in a business or research context.
