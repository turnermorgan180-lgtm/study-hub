Core quantitative rule

Chi-squared tests ask whether observed categorical patterns are too far from what the null model would predict.

Part 01

Two versions of chi-squared testing

The goodness-of-fit test checks whether observed counts match a claimed distribution. The test of association checks whether two categorical variables behave as if they are independent.

The formulas look similar because both compare observed and expected counts, but the source of the expected counts is different in each case. That distinction is central.

Part 02

Expected counts

Expected counts are what the null hypothesis predicts. In goodness-of-fit they come from stated proportions or a fair-process benchmark. In association tests they are calculated using row totals, column totals, and the grand total.

If you cannot explain where the expected numbers come from, you do not yet understand the test even if you can plug them into the formula.

Part 03

The statistic and degrees of freedom

The chi-squared statistic adds up the squared deviations between observed and expected counts, scaled by the expected counts. Large discrepancies relative to expectation produce a large test statistic.

Degrees of freedom matter because they determine the relevant critical value. In association tests the standard rule is (rows minus 1) times (columns minus 1).

Part 04

Validity conditions

The chi-squared approximation works best when expected counts are not too small. If too many cells have low expected counts, the test can become unreliable.

That means the best answers comment on assumptions and validity, not only on the raw test statistic or p-value.

Part 05

Association is not causation

A significant result in a test of association tells us that the variables are not behaving as if they are independent in the sample. It does not prove that one variable causes the other.

This matters particularly in business survey data, where omitted variables and confounding are common.

Part 06

How to write this in the exam

State the null, compute or explain the expected counts, give the test statistic, compare it with the critical rule, and then interpret the result in words. If the question is about association, say “evidence of association,” not “evidence of causation.”

Seminar angle

Week 6 gives exactly the table-based reasoning the exam likes: expected counts, degrees of freedom, and SPSS-style interpretation of significance and validity.

Common mistake

A frequent weak answer says “there is a relationship, so one variable affects the other.” The test does not justify that claim. It only supports non-independence in the observed categorical pattern.

Assessment-style prompt10 marks

Explain how a chi-squared test of association differs from a chi-squared goodness-of-fit test, and why expected counts matter in both.

Do not stop at calculation. Show the correct method, explain the logic, and then state what the result means in a business or research context.
