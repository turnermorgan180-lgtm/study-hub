Core quantitative rule

Model choice comes first. Bernoulli models one yes/no outcome, binomial models the count of successes, and the normal distribution models continuous outcomes around a mean.

Part 01

Why distributions matter

A distribution is a model for how outcomes behave across repeated observations. It gives more than a single probability. It gives the structure needed to calculate expected values, variances, cut-offs, and intervals in a coherent way.

In business applications, that structure lets us talk about defect counts, customer outcomes, weights, waiting times, and quality thresholds in a disciplined statistical language.

Part 02

Bernoulli and binomial logic

A Bernoulli variable has two outcomes, often labelled success and failure. A binomial variable counts how many successes occur over a fixed number of independent Bernoulli trials with constant success probability.

The mark-scoring step is to state the conditions before using the formula. If the number of trials is not fixed or the success probability is not stable, a binomial model may not be appropriate.

Part 03

Mean and variance as interpretation tools

The mean tells us the expected outcome, while the variance tells us how dispersed outcomes are around that expectation. For Bernoulli and binomial models, those formulas are compact and very examinable because they connect directly to risk and predictability.

A stronger answer explains what a larger variance means: more uncertainty, more spread, and less concentration around the expected value.

Part 04

Normal distribution and standardisation

The normal distribution matters because many business variables are approximately bell-shaped or because standardisation lets us work with normal tables for probability calculations.

A z-score translates a raw observation into a common scale. Once standardised, probabilities can be read from tables consistently, which is why the normal model becomes central across the rest of the course.

Part 05

Prediction intervals and thresholds

Prediction-style questions are often phrased as cut-offs: what proportion exceed a threshold, fall below a minimum, or lie inside a plausible range. The key method is to standardise first, then interpret the tail probability or interval correctly.

This is also where you must watch the direction of the question carefully. Below, above, within, and outside all require different probability statements.

Part 06

How to write this in the exam

State the model, define the parameters, standardise where needed, use the table, and interpret the answer as a probability, proportion, or expected count in context. Do not present the z-score as the final answer when the question asked for a probability.

Seminar angle

Week 3 combines direct calculations with interpretation of thresholds, which is exactly the sort of mixed quantitative reasoning the exam rewards.

Common mistake

Students often use the right formula on the wrong model. Counting successes is not the same as modelling one binary event, and a z-score is not itself a probability.

Assessment-style prompt10 marks

A food producer uses weight thresholds to classify items into standard and premium packs. Explain how the normal distribution helps this decision and what role the z-score plays.

Do not stop at calculation. Show the correct method, explain the logic, and then state what the result means in a business or research context.
