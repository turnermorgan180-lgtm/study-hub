Question 1

Most populous college

Full seminar question

Using a simple bar chart of College and frequencies, which college has the most people and by how much does it exceed the second-largest?

Model answerSeminar

The exact answer depends on the dataset opened in SPSS. A first-class response would state the graph result and then verify it using Analyze > Descriptive Statistics > Frequencies.

Question 2

Height distribution

Full seminar question

Using a histogram and boxplot of Height, is Height skewed or symmetric and what is the approximate average Height?

Model answerSeminar

The correct answer comes from inspecting the graph and then checking the exact mean in Descriptives. The strong method is to compare the visual impression with the numerical summary rather than relying on one alone.

Question 3

Gender by college

Full seminar question

Using clustered bar charts and crosstabs, how do men and women differ across colleges?

Model answerSeminar

The best answer identifies which colleges have more men or more women, then confirms the counts numerically using Crosstabs. The mark-scoring step is to move from graph to verified count.

Question 4

Breakfast and height

Full seminar question

What do population pyramids, boxplots, and Explore suggest about Height split by Breakfast?

Model answerSeminar

The stronger answer comments on centre, spread, overlap of confidence intervals, and whether any observed gap looks large relative to the underlying variation.

Question 5

Scatterplots and grouped scatterplots

Full seminar question

What do scatterplots of Height and Weight suggest, and how does grouping by Gender or Breakfast change the interpretation?

Model answerSeminar

The ideal answer links the overall positive relationship between height and weight to the possibility of group structure. This is also where the idea of controlling for a third variable becomes practically useful.

Question 6

Three-variable analysis

Full seminar question

How can you use SPSS to consider Height, Breakfast, and Gender all at once?

Model answerSeminar

Use grouped scatterplots, clustered boxplots, or Explore with factors so the third variable is not hidden. A strong answer explains that apparent effects can shrink or reverse once a confounding variable is considered.
