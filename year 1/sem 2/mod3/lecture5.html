Core quantitative rule

Hypothesis testing compares what the sample shows with what the null hypothesis would normally allow. The test statistic standardises that gap.

Test Logic
Decision rule
Step 1: state [[math:H_0]] and [[math:H_1]].
Step 2: compute test statistic using the appropriate standard error.
Step 3: compare with critical value or significance rule.
Step 4: reject or fail to reject and interpret.
Part 01

The purpose of a hypothesis test

A hypothesis test begins with a baseline claim, the null hypothesis, and asks whether the observed sample evidence is too unusual to treat as consistent with that baseline. This is the formal statistical version of asking whether the observed difference is just noise or something stronger.

In business terms, the null might say a defect rate has not changed, a training programme has no effect, or two customer groups do not differ. The alternative says there is a real difference worth taking seriously.

Part 02

Setting up [[math:H_0]] and [[math:H_1]]

A strong answer always states the null and alternative clearly before calculating. That matters because the direction of the alternative determines whether the test is one-tailed or two-tailed and therefore which critical value is appropriate.

If the setup is vague, the rest of the answer often collapses even if the arithmetic is neat.

Part 03

Test statistics and critical values

The test statistic measures how far the observed sample result sits from the null value once the standard error is taken into account. The larger the statistic, the harder it is to explain the sample under the null.

Critical values define the rejection boundary. If the statistic crosses that boundary, we reject the null at the chosen significance level. If it does not, we fail to reject it.

Part 04

Means, proportions, and paired data

Different contexts require different test setups. One-sample tests compare a sample against a benchmark. Two-sample tests compare independent groups. Paired tests compare before-and-after data or matched observations. Proportion tests use a different standard error logic from mean tests.

The most reliable exam strategy is to identify the data structure first and only then choose the formula.

Part 05

Significance versus importance

Rejecting the null means the data are unlikely under the null, not that the effect is necessarily large, valuable, or commercially decisive. A very small effect can be statistically significant with enough data.

That is why better answers distinguish statistical significance from business importance.

Part 06

How to conclude properly

The final sentence should say whether the null is rejected or not, at what significance level, and what that means in plain context. “Reject [[math:H_0]]” on its own is incomplete. The interpretation sentence is part of the quantitative answer.

Common mistake

Students often compute a test statistic correctly but then interpret failure to reject the null as proof that the null is true. That is too strong. It only means the evidence is not strong enough to reject it.

Assessment-style prompt10 marks

Explain why rejecting a null hypothesis is not the same as proving that a managerial intervention has a large practical effect.

Do not stop at calculation. Show the correct method, explain the logic, and then state what the result means in a business or research context.
