Core quantitative rule

Confidence interval = estimate ± critical value x standard error. Standard error falls with sample size, so larger samples usually give more precise inference.

Part 01

Population, sample, and inference

Inferential statistics exists because decision-makers usually cannot observe the entire population. Instead they observe a sample and use it to estimate or test something about the wider population.

This creates an unavoidable uncertainty gap. Confidence intervals and statistical tests are the formal tools used to manage that uncertainty rather than ignore it.

Part 02

Sampling design and data quality

The quality of inference starts before any formula appears. Random sampling supports generalisation because every unit has a known and ideally equal chance of selection. Poorly designed surveys, convenience samples, and leading questions can bias the evidence before analysis begins.

A strong answer therefore treats sampling design as part of quantitative analysis, not just as background detail.

Part 03

The Central Limit Theorem

The Central Limit Theorem is one of the key bridges between raw data and inference. It says that for sufficiently large samples, the distribution of sample means is approximately normal even if the original population is not.

That matters because normality of the sampling distribution makes interval estimation and many test procedures feasible. The theorem is not saying the original data become normal. It is about the behaviour of sample means across repeated samples.

Part 04

Standard error and precision

The standard error measures how much a sample statistic would typically vary from sample to sample. It is smaller than the standard deviation because it reflects uncertainty in the estimate, not raw variation in the underlying observations.

The exam-relevant consequence is simple: larger samples reduce the standard error, which tightens intervals and strengthens precision.

Part 05

Confidence intervals for means and proportions

A confidence interval combines an estimate with a margin of error. The margin depends on the critical value and the standard error. Higher confidence increases the critical value and makes the interval wider. Larger samples decrease the standard error and make the interval narrower.

For means and proportions alike, a stronger answer states what the interval suggests about plausible population values rather than just quoting the two endpoints.

Part 06

Sample-size design

Sample-size formulas reverse the confidence-interval logic. Instead of starting with data and asking how precise the interval is, they start with the desired precision and confidence level and ask how much data are needed.

This is highly practical in business settings because it links research design to resource planning. The cost of collecting more observations is traded off against the gain in precision.

Seminar angle

Week 4 is one of the most repeatedly useful weeks because confidence intervals, sample-size questions, and CLT interpretation all recur naturally in coursework and exam settings.

Common mistake

The most common error is to say a 95 percent confidence interval means there is a 95 percent chance the true value lies in that one interval. The correct interpretation is about the reliability of the method across repeated samples.
