Key Definitions

Terms to Know Cold

Market failure
A situation where the free market fails to allocate resources in a socially efficient way.
Social efficiency
Allocative efficiency from society’s point of view; achieved where marginal social benefit equals marginal social cost.
Equity
Fairness in the distribution of income, consumption, opportunity, or welfare.
Externality
A cost or benefit affecting a third party not directly involved in the market transaction.
Marginal private cost (MPC)
The extra cost borne by the producer or consumer making the decision.
Marginal social cost (MSC)
The full extra cost to society: MPC plus any marginal external cost.
Marginal private benefit (MPB)
The benefit enjoyed by the consumer or producer making the decision.
Marginal social benefit (MSB)
The full extra benefit to society: MPB plus any marginal external benefit.
Deadweight welfare loss
The loss of total consumer-plus-producer surplus caused by output being above or below the socially optimal level.
Laissez-faire
The view that markets should be left largely alone, with minimal government intervention.
01

Why Governments Intervene

Government intervention begins from a simple idea: markets only work well when the decisions made by private individuals and firms line up with the interests of society as a whole. If private incentives and social welfare point in the same direction, the invisible hand works. If they diverge, the market can misallocate resources.

Core rule

Social efficiency is achieved where MSB = MSC. If the last unit brings more benefit than cost, society wants more of it. If it creates more cost than benefit, society wants less of it.

The lecture gives two broad reasons for intervention. First, efficiency: government may act because the free market produces too much or too little of a good. Second, equity: even if a market outcome is efficient, it may still be socially unacceptable because it produces very unequal outcomes.

  • Efficiency motive: correct market failure and move output toward the socially optimal level.
  • Equity motive: redistribute resources when the market delivers outcomes that are efficient but unfair.
Exam insight

Do not define intervention as “government getting involved.” That is too weak. The proper answer is that government intervenes when free markets fail to deliver socially efficient or equitable outcomes.

02

Externalities: The Core Logic of Market Failure

Externalities exist whenever part of the cost or benefit of an action falls on someone other than the decision-maker. That matters because market participants usually respond only to their own private incentives. If a firm dumps waste into a river, it considers its production cost, not the health cost imposed on the public. If a person gets vaccinated, they consider their own benefit, not the protection given to others.

Relationships

MSC = MPC + MEC
MSB = MPB + MEB

These relationships let you classify the problem immediately:

Case Relationship Market result Social implication
Negative externality MSC > MPC Overproduction Too many resources allocated
Positive externality MSC < MPC Underproduction Too few resources allocated
Negative consumption externality MSB < MPB Overconsumption Society gets too much of the activity
Positive consumption externality MSB > MPB Underconsumption Society gets too little of the activity
Common mistake

Students often memorise “negative = bad” and “positive = good” but then fail to connect that to output. The mark-scoring step is stating whether the market produces too much or too little relative to the social optimum.

03

Market Power and Deadweight Welfare Loss

Externalities are not the only source of market failure. Market power also matters. Under perfect competition, firms are price takers and the equilibrium tends toward the socially efficient level where price reflects marginal cost. Under monopoly, however, the firm restricts output to raise price and maximise profit.

The lecture’s logic is very direct. Assume there are no externalities, so:

  • MC = MSC
  • Price / AR = MSB

Now compare the outcomes:

  • Monopolist: chooses output where MR = MC → output Q1
  • Social optimum: requires MSB = MSC → output Q2

Because the monopolist faces a downward-sloping demand curve, MR lies below AR. That means the monopoly output is lower than the socially efficient output. Society gets too little output and pays too high a price.

How to describe the diagram in an exam
  • Vertical axis: costs, benefits, price, revenue.
  • Horizontal axis: quantity.
  • AR = demand = MSB, downward sloping.
  • MR lies below AR.
  • MC = MSC under the no-externality assumption.
  • The monopolist produces at MR = MC, giving Q1 and price P1 from the demand curve.
  • The socially efficient output is where AR = MC, giving Q2.
  • Therefore Q1 < Q2 and a deadweight welfare loss exists between Q1 and Q2.

Deadweight welfare loss is not just “high prices.” It is the loss of total surplus — consumer plus producer surplus — relative to the competitive benchmark.

04

Negative Production Externalities (MSC > MPC)

This occurs when production imposes uncompensated costs on others. The classic examples are pollution, noise, congestion, environmental damage, or public health costs created by a production process.

The logic

The firm makes decisions based on its own marginal private cost. But society bears an additional marginal external cost. Therefore the true cost to society is higher than the cost seen by the firm.

Relationship

MSC = MPC + MECp

1
Free market equilibrium: output is set where MPB = MPC, giving quantity Q1.
2
Social perspective: the real cost is MSC, which lies above MPC.
3
Social optimum: occurs where MSB = MSC, giving quantity Q2.
4
Conclusion: Q1 > Q2, so the market overproduces.

The missing-market idea matters here. No one “owns” the air or river, so the producer can use them as free dumping grounds. That is why the firm’s private cost understates the social cost.

Evaluation

In reality, measuring the exact external cost is difficult. Governments may know pollution exists, but not the precise monetary value of the damage. That makes it hard to set the perfectly corrective tax.

05

Positive Production Externalities (MSC < MPC)

This occurs when production creates benefits for third parties that the producer is not paid for. The lecture example is tree planting: a timber company benefits from selling wood, but society also benefits from carbon absorption and environmental improvement.

Relationship

MSC = MPC − MEBp

That means the social cost curve lies below the private cost curve. The firm sees production as more costly than it really is from society’s perspective, because it is not rewarded for the external benefit it generates.

  • Market equilibrium: MPB = MPC → Q1
  • Social optimum: MSB = MSC → Q2
  • Therefore: Q1 < Q2

The result is underproduction. Society would like more of the activity than the market supplies.

Common mistake

Do not say “positive externalities make firms produce more.” That is wrong in the market-failure context. The issue is that although the activity is socially beneficial, the firm does not capture the full benefit, so it produces too little.

06

Negative Consumption Externalities (MSB < MPB)

This occurs when an individual gains private benefit from consuming a good, but imposes costs on others. Examples include alcohol abuse, passive smoking, reckless driving, or mobile-phone use while driving.

Here the problem is on the benefit side, not the cost side. The consumer values the good more highly than society does because they ignore the harm caused to third parties.

Relationship

MSB = MPB − MECc

  • No production externality assumed, so MSC = MPC.
  • Market equilibrium occurs where MPB = MSC, giving Q1.
  • Social optimum occurs where MSB = MSC, giving Q2.
  • Because MSB lies below MPB, Q1 > Q2.

The market overconsumes the good relative to the socially desirable level.

Exam wording
  • Demand curve represents MPB.
  • MSB lies below demand because external costs reduce the benefit to society.
  • Supply curve represents MSC when no production externality is assumed.
  • The free market chooses Q1, while the social optimum is Q2.
  • Hence the good is overconsumed and there is welfare loss.
07

Positive Consumption Externalities (MSB > MPB)

This occurs when the consumer enjoys a private benefit, but others also gain. Vaccination is the standard example: the individual gets protection, but society also benefits from reduced disease transmission.

Relationship

MSB = MPB + MEBc

The demand curve reflects only MPB. The true social benefit curve lies above it. As a result, consumers buy too little relative to the socially efficient amount.

  • Market equilibrium: MPB = MSC → Q1
  • Social optimum: MSB = MSC → Q2
  • Therefore: Q1 < Q2

This is why governments often subsidise education, healthcare, public transport, or vaccination: they want to push consumption toward the higher social optimum.

Exam insight

When discussing merit goods, do not stop at “they are good for you.” The stronger answer is that the market underconsumes them because private decision-makers ignore wider social benefits.

08

Policies to Correct Market Failure

Once you identify whether the problem is overproduction, underproduction, overconsumption, or underconsumption, the policy logic becomes clearer. Government aims to move the market quantity toward the social optimum.

Problem Policy direction Typical tool
Negative externality Reduce quantity Indirect tax, regulation, legal penalties
Positive externality Increase quantity Subsidy, public provision, information campaigns
Monopoly power Increase output / reduce price distortion Competition policy, regulation, public ownership
Equity problem Redistribute access or income Transfers, progressive taxation, public services

Indirect taxes

Used mainly for negative externalities. A tax raises the private cost faced by the firm or consumer, pushing private decision-making closer to the true social cost.

Subsidies

Used mainly for positive externalities. A subsidy lowers private cost or raises private benefit, encouraging more production or consumption.

Regulation

Rather than changing incentives through prices, regulation directly restricts behaviour. Examples include pollution limits, smoking bans, compulsory education, or safety laws.

Public provision

Sometimes government produces or funds the good directly, especially where underconsumption is severe or private provision is insufficient.

Common mistake

Never discuss policy without linking it to the original distortion. For example: “A subsidy increases output” is incomplete. The full chain is: positive externality → market underproduces → subsidy lowers private cost / raises private benefit → output rises toward Q2.

09

The Case for Laissez-faire

The lecture does not present intervention as automatically correct. There is also a case for leaving markets alone. Supporters of laissez-faire argue that governments may lack the information, incentives, or administrative ability to improve on market outcomes.

  • Governments may not know the exact size of an external cost or benefit.
  • Policies can be blunt and create unintended side effects.
  • Bureaucracy and compliance costs may reduce efficiency.
  • Political pressures may distort intervention toward special interests.
High-level evaluation

The strongest judgement is balanced: market failure justifies intervention in principle, but government failure means the real question is not “intervene or not?” It is “which policy is least imperfect?”

10

Exam Questions and Model Answers

Section A style question 2 marks

In a perfect market, social efficiency in any activity will be maximised where the activity’s:

A. marginal benefit equals marginal cost.

B. total benefit equals total cost.

C. marginal social benefit exceeds marginal social cost.

D. marginal social benefit equals marginal social cost.

E. total social benefit exceeds total social cost.

Answer

D. marginal social benefit equals marginal social cost.

This is the precise condition for allocative efficiency from society’s point of view. The optimum is where the last unit produced or consumed adds exactly as much benefit to society as it adds cost.

Section A style question 2 marks

Which one of the following is a definition of deadweight welfare loss under monopoly?

A. The loss in output compared with perfect competition.

B. The increase in price compared with perfect competition.

C. The loss of total consumer surplus compared with perfect competition.

D. The increase in total profit compared with perfect competition.

E. The loss of total consumer-plus-producer surplus compared with perfect competition.

Answer

E. The loss of total consumer-plus-producer surplus compared with perfect competition.

Deadweight loss is the lost welfare that disappears entirely when output is restricted below the socially efficient level. It is not just higher prices or lower consumer surplus on their own.

Section B style question 8 marks

Explain why a negative production externality leads to overproduction in a free market.

Model answer

A negative production externality occurs when production imposes a cost on third parties that the producer does not pay for. As a result, the producer’s marginal private cost is lower than the true marginal social cost. In diagram terms, the MSC curve lies above the MPC curve.

In a free market, the firm chooses output where marginal private benefit equals marginal private cost. This gives market equilibrium quantity Q1. However, the socially efficient level of output is where MSB equals MSC, which is a lower quantity Q2.

Because the producer ignores the external cost, the free market output exceeds the social optimum. Therefore Q1 is greater than Q2 and the good is overproduced. The extra units between Q2 and Q1 create more cost to society than benefit, which generates a deadweight welfare loss.

Section B style question 10 marks

Explain how a subsidy might be used to correct a positive consumption externality.

Model answer

A positive consumption externality exists when consuming a good creates benefits for third parties that are not taken into account by the individual consumer. This means marginal social benefit exceeds marginal private benefit, so the MSB curve lies above the MPB curve.

In the free market, consumers choose quantity where MPB equals MSC. This gives a market quantity Q1. However, the socially efficient level of consumption is where MSB equals MSC, which is a higher quantity Q2. The market therefore underconsumes the good.

A subsidy can be used to encourage additional consumption by lowering the effective price paid by consumers or by increasing the private benefit they receive. This shifts private incentives closer to social incentives and increases consumption toward Q2. If the subsidy is well targeted, it reduces the welfare loss caused by underconsumption.

Examples include subsidies for education, vaccines, or public transport, where the private user does not capture the full benefit to society.

11

Evaluation and Common Mistakes

Evaluation points to use in essays

Measurement problem: Governments rarely know the exact size of external costs or benefits, so policy may overshoot or undershoot.

Administrative cost: Taxes, subsidies, and regulation can be expensive to implement and monitor.

Unintended consequences: For example, heavy taxation may create black markets or evasion.

Government failure: Intervention may be distorted by politics, lobbying, or weak information.

Time horizon: Some benefits from intervention, such as education or environmental protection, appear only in the long run.

Top mistakes

1. Confusing private and social curves.

2. Forgetting to say whether output is too high or too low.

3. Describing a policy without linking it back to the original distortion.

4. Defining deadweight loss as “high prices” rather than lost total surplus.

5. Assuming intervention is automatically good without evaluating government failure.

One-line memory aid

Negative externality = market does too much.
Positive externality = market does too little.
Government tries to move Q from the market level to the social optimum where MSB = MSC.
