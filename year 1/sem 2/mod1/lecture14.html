Part 01

Trade Patterns and the Growth of World Trade

International trade is the exchange of goods and services across national borders. For business economics, the key point is that firms no longer compete only within domestic markets. Prices, output decisions, input sourcing, and competitive pressure are shaped by global demand and supply conditions.

The lecture begins by stressing that world trade has grown faster than world output for long periods. That means economies have become more open: a larger share of what countries produce is exported, and a larger share of what they consume uses imported goods, components, or services.

Trade openness

Trade openness means a rising importance of exports and imports in national income. A more open economy is more exposed to foreign competition, foreign demand, and global shocks.

Trade patterns are not evenly distributed. Some countries are major exporters of manufactures, some specialise in commodities, some dominate commercial services, and the geography of trade changes over time as emerging economies industrialise and supply chains shift.

Pattern What it means Business implication
Trade growth Exports and imports rise faster than GDP Domestic firms face stronger international competition
Changing geography Different regions gain or lose trade share over time Location decisions and market strategy become dynamic
Shift to services Commercial services become more important Trade is not only about physical goods
Exam insight

Do not define international trade narrowly as “countries buying from each other.” The stronger answer links trade to specialisation, comparative cost differences, and integration of national economies.

Part 02

Why Countries Trade

The core reason countries trade is that they are not identical. They differ in resource endowments, technology, climate, skills, scale, and opportunity costs. Trade allows countries to specialise more in activities where they are relatively efficient and obtain other goods more cheaply through exchange.

Specialisation

Specialisation means concentrating production in the goods or services where a country has a relative cost advantage. It does not mean producing only one thing; it means shifting resources toward areas of stronger relative efficiency.

Absolute advantage
A country can produce more output with the same resources, or the same output with fewer resources, than another country.
Comparative advantage
A country has a lower opportunity cost in producing one good relative to another good.
Opportunity cost
The amount of the next best alternative that must be given up to produce an extra unit of a good.
Intra-industry trade
Countries both import and export similar categories of goods, often because of product differentiation and economies of scale.

The lecture’s logic is that trade is beneficial even when one country is absolutely better at producing everything, provided comparative costs still differ. That is why comparative advantage, not absolute advantage, is the main theorem students are expected to remember.

Part 03

Comparative Advantage: The Core Exam Concept

A country has a comparative advantage in good X compared with good Y if it can produce X at a lower opportunity cost than another country. This is the wording exam questions typically test.

Rule to memorise

Comparative advantage is about relative sacrifice, not absolute productivity. Ask what must be given up to produce one more unit.

1
Find the production possibilities or unit labour requirements. You need data for two goods.
2
Compute opportunity cost. How many units of good Y are forgone to produce one more unit of good X?
3
Compare countries. The lower opportunity cost producer has the comparative advantage.
4
Predict specialisation. Each country specialises relatively more in the good where it has comparative advantage.
Question Wrong approach Correct approach
Who should export wine? Choose the country that simply makes more wine Choose the country with the lower opportunity cost of wine
Who should export cloth? Choose the country with the lower labour hours in isolation Compare labour hours relative to the alternative good
What matters? Absolute efficiency only Relative efficiency across goods
Common mistake

Students often identify the country that is “best overall” and assume it should export everything. That misses the point. Comparative advantage is about relative efficiency, so both countries can gain if each specialises according to lower opportunity cost.

Part 04

Gains from Trade and Their Limits

If countries specialise according to comparative advantage and then trade, total world output can rise. The gain comes from reallocating resources away from relatively inefficient uses and toward relatively efficient ones.

Static gains

  • More output from existing resources because production is allocated more efficiently.
  • Lower prices and greater consumer choice from access to imports.
  • Competitive pressure on domestic firms to improve efficiency.

Dynamic gains

  • Greater economies of scale as firms serve larger markets.
  • More innovation through stronger competition and knowledge spillovers.
  • Access to a wider range of inputs, technologies, and capital goods.

The lecture also points out that gains from trade are not unlimited. Specialisation can be constrained by transport costs, changing technology, strategic dependence on key imports, imperfect labour mobility, and the fact that countries may not want to become over-dependent on a narrow export base.

Balanced judgement

The theory predicts aggregate gains from trade, but those gains may be unevenly distributed. Some firms, workers, or regions gain strongly while others lose from import competition. That is one reason protection remains politically attractive even when total welfare falls.

Part 05

Terms of Trade

The terms of trade measure the price of a country’s exports relative to the price of its imports. They show how many imports a country can obtain for a given volume of exports.

Formula

Terms of trade index = export price index / import price index × 100

If the terms of trade improve, export prices rise relative to import prices. That means a country can obtain more imports for the same quantity of exports. If the terms of trade deteriorate, the opposite happens.

Movement Meaning Likely effect
Improvement Export prices rise relative to import prices Purchasing power of exports increases
Deterioration Import prices rise relative to export prices Country must export more to buy the same imports

However, better terms of trade are not always unambiguously good. If export prices rise because domestic costs are becoming uncompetitive, export volumes may fall. Equally, a worsening terms of trade may coincide with higher competitiveness and stronger export growth if export prices fall because productivity has improved.

Part 06

Arguments for Restricting Trade

The lecture then turns from the free-trade case to the case for protection. These arguments matter because exam questions often ask whether trade restrictions can be justified in principle, even if they reduce allocative efficiency.

  • Infant industry argument: new domestic industries may need temporary protection while they learn, build scale, and reduce average cost.
  • Employment argument: import competition may reduce jobs in vulnerable sectors.
  • Strategic or national security argument: countries may want domestic capacity in food, energy, defence, or critical technology.
  • Anti-dumping argument: protection may be used against foreign firms selling below cost to gain market share.
  • Balance of payments argument: countries facing persistent external deficits may want to reduce import demand.

These arguments can be economically coherent, but the key issue is whether governments can identify the right industries and remove protection once the original justification disappears.

Common mistake

Do not write that protection is justified simply because “imports are bad for domestic firms.” Competition harming inefficient firms is not, by itself, an economic justification. You need a market-failure, strategic, or adjustment-cost argument.

Part 07

Methods of Restricting Trade

Protection can be imposed in several ways. The lecture distinguishes between price-based restrictions and quantity-based restrictions.

Method Mechanism Main effect
Tariff Tax on imports Raises domestic price and reduces imports
Quota Direct quantitative limit on imports Restricts import volume regardless of exact demand
Subsidy Support to domestic producers Improves domestic competitiveness without directly taxing imports
Administrative barriers Standards, customs delays, regulations Raises import costs indirectly

A tariff is the most diagram-heavy method, so it tends to be the one used in exam questions. A tariff pushes domestic price above the world price, encourages domestic production, reduces domestic consumption, and compresses imports.

Tariff diagram logic
  • World price under free trade is below the domestic equilibrium price.
  • Consumers buy at the world price, so imports fill the gap between domestic demand and domestic supply.
  • A tariff raises domestic price from Pw to Pt.
  • At the higher price, domestic supply rises and domestic demand falls.
  • Imports shrink because the gap between demand and supply becomes smaller.
Part 08

Tariffs, Welfare Effects, and Efficiency Loss

The standard tariff diagram is really a welfare diagram. It shows who gains, who loses, and why the country as a whole usually suffers a net efficiency loss from protection.

Who gains and who loses?

  • Domestic producers gain because they receive a higher price and sell more output.
  • Government gains tariff revenue on remaining imports.
  • Consumers lose because they pay a higher price and consume less.

The consumer loss is larger than the producer gain plus tariff revenue. The missing amount is the deadweight efficiency loss.

Net welfare result

Net cost of a tariff = production distortion loss + consumption distortion loss

The production distortion occurs because higher-cost domestic output replaces lower-cost imports. The consumption distortion occurs because some mutually beneficial consumption disappears when price rises.

1
Free trade price = Pw. Imports are bought from the world market at the lower price.
2
Tariff raises price to Pt. Consumers contract demand and firms expand domestic supply.
3
Transfer effects occur. Some consumer surplus moves to producers and government.
4
Efficiency loss remains. Two deadweight triangles represent the net national loss.
Important qualification

For a small country that cannot influence world prices, the standard result is a net welfare loss. For a large country, a tariff might improve the terms of trade by pushing foreign exporters to lower prices, but that argument is much more conditional and can trigger retaliation.

Common mistake

Do not say “tariff revenue means the tariff is beneficial overall.” Tariff revenue is only a transfer within the country. The mark-scoring point is that the two distortion losses remain after transfers are accounted for.

Part 09

The World Trading System and the WTO

The lecture ends with the rules-based trading system. The World Trade Organization replaced the post-war GATT framework in 1995 and provides a legal and institutional structure for negotiating trade rules, reducing barriers, and settling disputes.

GATT
General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, the post-1947 framework for reducing trade barriers.
WTO
World Trade Organization, established in 1995 to govern trade rules, negotiations, and dispute settlement.
MFN
Most-favoured-nation treatment: members should not discriminate between trading partners.
National treatment
Imported goods should be treated no less favourably than domestic goods once inside the market.

The broad economic case for the WTO is that rules reduce uncertainty. Firms are more willing to trade and invest when tariff commitments, dispute procedures, and non-discrimination principles are clearer.

Function Why it matters
Negotiation forum Allows countries to bargain over lower trade barriers in formal rounds
Rule framework Creates common expectations over tariffs, subsidies, and trade procedures
Dispute settlement Reduces the risk that trade conflicts escalate into unilateral retaliation

However, the system is not frictionless. Trade rounds can stall, enforcement can be politically difficult, and critics argue that the benefits of liberalisation are unevenly distributed across countries and sectors.

Part 10

Exam Questions, Model Answers, and Final Evaluation

Section A style 2 marks

A country has a comparative advantage in producing good X if it can produce X:

A. with fewer workers than any other country.

B. at a lower opportunity cost than another country.

C. at a lower money wage than another country.

D. at a higher output level than another country.

Answer

B. at a lower opportunity cost than another country.

This is the precise definition. Comparative advantage is about relative sacrifice, not simply greater productivity.

Section A style 2 marks

If a tariff on an imported good is abolished and domestic price falls from Pt to Pw, what is the net efficiency gain to the country?

A. The increase in producer surplus only.

B. Tariff revenue only.

C. The two distortion triangles previously caused by the tariff.

D. The entire increase in consumer surplus.

Answer

C. The two distortion triangles previously caused by the tariff.

Transfers between consumers, producers, and government cancel out at the national level. The genuine efficiency gain is the removal of the production and consumption deadweight losses.

Section B style 8 marks

Explain why countries can gain from trade even if one country has an absolute advantage in producing every good.

Model answer

Absolute advantage means one country can produce every good more efficiently in direct productivity terms. But this does not remove the possibility of trade gains, because what matters for specialisation is comparative advantage, not absolute advantage.

Comparative advantage depends on opportunity cost. Even if one country is better at producing both goods, it will usually be relatively better at one of them. The other country will then have the lower opportunity cost in the second good. If each country specialises more in the good where its relative efficiency is greater, total world output can rise.

After specialisation, the countries trade. Because the terms of exchange lie between their domestic opportunity costs, both can consume beyond what they could achieve in autarky. Therefore both can gain from trade even when one country has an absolute advantage in all goods.

Section B style 10 marks

Evaluate the case for imposing a tariff on imported goods.

Model answer

A tariff is a tax on imports. It raises the domestic price of the imported good, reduces consumption, encourages domestic production, and cuts the volume of imports. Domestic producers gain and the government receives tariff revenue, but consumers lose from higher prices and reduced choice.

For a small country, the standard welfare analysis shows that the tariff causes a net efficiency loss. This arises from a production distortion, because higher-cost domestic production replaces lower-cost imports, and a consumption distortion, because some mutually beneficial consumption no longer takes place.

There are arguments in favour of tariffs in specific cases. Temporary protection may help infant industries grow, and strategic sectors may be protected for national security reasons. Governments may also use tariffs against dumping or during severe balance of payments pressure.

However, these arguments are conditional. Governments may protect the wrong industries, firms may become inefficient behind trade barriers, consumers bear the cost, and other countries may retaliate. Therefore the strongest evaluation is that tariffs can sometimes be defended in narrow circumstances, but as a general policy they reduce efficiency and lower national welfare.

High-level evaluation points

Comparative advantage is powerful but simplified: it abstracts from transport costs, strategic behaviour, and adjustment problems.

Trade raises aggregate welfare, not guaranteed equal welfare: some workers and regions can lose badly in the short run.

Protection has visible beneficiaries and diffuse costs: producer gains are concentrated, but consumer losses are spread widely.

Tariff diagrams assume a small country: if a country can affect world prices, the analysis becomes less straightforward.

WTO rules reduce uncertainty but do not eliminate conflict: trade politics and stalled negotiations still matter.

Top mistakes

1. Confusing comparative advantage with absolute advantage.

2. Saying “trade is good because exports rise” without explaining efficiency and specialisation.

3. Forgetting that terms of trade are a price ratio, not the trade balance.

4. Treating tariff revenue as if it removes the deadweight loss.

5. Listing protectionist arguments without evaluating whether they are temporary, targeted, or politically abused.

One-line memory aid

Free trade is justified by comparative advantage and higher total welfare; protection is defended only where there is a strong argument that market outcomes, adjustment costs, or strategic interests make unrestricted trade too costly.
