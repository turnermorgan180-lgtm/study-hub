Key Definitions

Terms to Know Cold

Financial intermediation
The process by which banks and financial institutions channel funds from savers to borrowers.
Liquidity
The ease with which an asset can be used for transactions without loss of value.
Money supply
The stock of money available in the economy.
Interest rate
The price of borrowing or the reward for lending.
Monetary transmission
The chain through which changes in money and interest rates affect spending and output.
Credit creation
Expansion of deposits through bank lending.
Part 01

The Financial System Matches Savers and Borrowers

The financial system exists because many households want to save while firms and governments want to borrow. Banks, bond markets, and other institutions reduce transaction costs, spread risk, and make it easier for funds to move from surplus units to deficit units.

For business economics, the key implication is that investment spending depends not just on business confidence but on whether finance is available and at what interest rate.

Part 02

What Makes Money Special?

Money is a medium of exchange, a store of value, and a unit of account. These functions matter because firms need liquidity for transactions, households hold money for convenience, and contracts are written in monetary terms.

Core rule

People hold money because it is liquid, but they give up the interest they could have earned on other assets. That trade-off is central to money demand.

Part 03

Banks, Deposits and Credit Creation

Commercial banks do more than store cash. By making loans, they create deposits and therefore expand the money stock used in transactions. That does not mean banks can lend without limit, but it does mean the supply of money depends partly on lending behaviour and regulation.

Part 04

Why Do People Demand Money?

Money demand is usually explained through transaction, precautionary, and asset motives. Higher income raises the need for transactions balances. Higher interest rates reduce the desire to hold money because the opportunity cost of liquidity rises.

Exam insight

The stronger answer says money demand is positively related to income and negatively related to the interest rate. That is the standard comparative-statics statement to remember.

Part 05

Interest Rates Are Determined by Money Demand and Money Supply

In the simplest money-market framework, the interest rate adjusts to equate money demand with money supply. If the central bank increases money supply, there is excess liquidity at the original rate, so interest rates tend to fall. If money demand rises for a given supply, rates tend to rise.

In diagram terms
  • Put interest rate on the vertical axis and quantity of money on the horizontal axis.
  • Draw money supply vertical in the simple model.
  • Draw money demand downward sloping because higher interest rates make holding money less attractive.
  • A rightward shift in money supply lowers the equilibrium interest rate.
Part 06

The Interest-Rate Transmission Mechanism

1
Money supply rises or policy rates fall.
2
Market interest rates tend to fall.
3
Borrowing becomes cheaper. Investment and interest-sensitive consumption may rise.
4
Aggregate demand increases. Output and employment may expand.

That chain also affects exchange rates, asset prices, and confidence. Lower rates can weaken the currency, support exports, and reinforce demand.

Part 07

Evaluation and Common Pitfalls

Common mistakes

Do not say lower interest rates always create a large rise in investment. Firms may still hesitate if confidence is weak.

Do not treat money supply as identical to physical cash. Most money in modern economies exists as bank deposits.

The transmission mechanism can weaken when banks are unwilling to lend, when firms are pessimistic, or when households prefer to save rather than spend. The stronger evaluation therefore stresses that interest rates matter, but they do not operate in isolation.

Part 08

Exam Practice

Section B Style Prompt12 marks

Explain how an increase in the money supply could affect business activity.

Start with the money market: greater money supply lowers interest rates if money demand is unchanged. Then explain cheaper borrowing, stronger investment, possible exchange-rate effects, and higher aggregate demand. Finish by evaluating confidence, banking conditions, and inflation constraints.
