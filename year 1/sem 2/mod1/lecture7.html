Key Definitions

Terms to Know Cold

Market structure
The competitive environment in which firms operate, shaped by the number of firms, entry conditions, and product characteristics.
Price taker
A firm with no control over market price; it accepts the price determined by the market.
Price maker
A firm with some control over price because it faces a downward-sloping demand curve.
Perfect competition
A theoretical market structure with many firms, free entry and exit, homogeneous products, and perfect knowledge.
Monopoly
A market with a single seller, usually protected by substantial barriers to entry.
Marginal revenue (MR)
The extra revenue gained from selling one more unit of output.
Supernormal profit
Profit above normal profit; earned when average revenue exceeds average cost at the chosen output.
Normal profit
The minimum profit required to keep a firm in its current industry in the long run.
Barrier to entry
Any obstacle that makes it difficult or costly for new firms to enter a market.
Contestable market
A market in which the threat of entry is strong enough to discipline incumbent firms, even if few firms currently operate.
01

Market Structure: The Spectrum from Competitive to Concentrated

The lecture begins with a simple idea: firm behaviour is not random. It depends on the degree of competition in the market. If a firm faces many rivals and easy entry, it behaves very differently from a firm protected by strong barriers and weak substitutes.

The four structures presented are perfect competition, monopolistic competition, oligopoly, and monopoly. They run from most competitive to least competitive. The lecture’s emphasis is on the two endpoints because they make the underlying logic clearest.

Structure Firms Entry Product Demand curve faced by firm
Perfect competition Very many Unrestricted Homogeneous Horizontal; firm is a price taker
Monopolistic competition Many Unrestricted Differentiated Downward sloping but relatively elastic
Oligopoly Few Restricted Can be homogeneous or differentiated Downward sloping; shape depends on rival reactions
Monopoly One Restricted or blocked Unique Downward sloping and relatively inelastic
Exam insight

When comparing market structures, do not stop at listing features. The marks usually come from linking structure to pricing power, elasticity of demand, and profit conditions.

02

What Determines the Degree of Competition?

The lecture identifies four practical determinants of competition. These explain why some firms constantly innovate and promote, while others can remain dominant with far less pressure.

1
Number of firms: more firms usually means more rivalry for customers and market share.
2
Freedom of entry and exit: if new firms can enter easily, incumbents cannot rely on protected profits for long.
3
Nature of the product: homogeneous products push competition toward price, while differentiation gives firms some market power.
4
Shape of the demand curve: the more elastic the demand faced by the firm, the less control it has over price.
Core idea

Competition is not just about the number of firms. It is also about how easy it is to enter, how close substitutes are, and how strongly buyers react to price changes.

This is why Tesco is not a monopoly even if it is large. It operates in an oligopolistic setting because other major supermarkets exist and consumer demand can shift across rivals.

03

Perfect Competition: The Theoretical Ideal

Perfect competition is presented as a benchmark rather than a common real-world market. It helps economists understand what happens when no firm has any individual market power.

The four core assumptions

  • Price takers: each firm is so small relative to the market that it cannot influence price.
  • Freedom of entry and exit: there are no barriers preventing firms entering in the long run.
  • Homogeneous products: products are identical, so consumers are indifferent between sellers.
  • Perfect knowledge: consumers and producers know prices, quality, and available techniques.
Under perfect competition, the individual firm faces a perfectly elastic demand curve at the market price. That gives the standard relationship P = AR = MR.

The key implication is immediate. If one firm tries to charge above the market price, buyers switch instantly to identical rivals. If it charges below, it sacrifices revenue unnecessarily because it could have sold the same output at the market price.

04

Short-Run Equilibrium in Perfect Competition

In the short run, the number of firms is fixed. Existing firms cannot be joined immediately by new rivals, so any supernormal profit can persist temporarily.

Price determination

The lecture separates the industry from the firm. The industry price is set by market demand and market supply. The individual firm then takes that price as given.

Profit maximisation

Rule

The firm maximises profit where MR = MC. Under perfect competition, that means P = AR = MR = MC at the chosen output.

If average revenue lies above average cost at that output, the firm earns supernormal profit. The profit area is the usual rectangle: (AR - AC) × Q.

How to Describe the Short-Run Diagram
  • Industry equilibrium gives the market price Pe.
  • The firm faces a horizontal AR = MR line at Pe.
  • The firm chooses output where MC cuts MR.
  • Read AC at that output to judge whether profit, loss, or normal profit is being made.
Common mistake

Students often say firms in perfect competition produce where price equals average cost. That is only the long-run equilibrium condition. In the short run, the actual decision rule is still MR = MC.

05

Long-Run Equilibrium in Perfect Competition

The lecture then explains the classic adjustment process. Supernormal profits attract new firms. As entry occurs, industry supply shifts right, the market price falls, and incumbent firms lose their excess profit.

1
Supernormal profit exists. Existing firms are earning more than normal return.
2
Entry occurs. New firms are attracted because barriers are absent.
3
Industry supply increases. The market supply curve shifts right.
4
Price falls. Each firm’s AR = MR line shifts downward.
5
Only normal profit remains. Entry stops once there is no longer any incentive to join.
Long-run equilibrium condition

LRAC = AC = MC = MR = AR

This means the firm operates at the minimum point of long-run average cost, so productive efficiency is achieved. It is one reason perfect competition is often treated as a benchmark for efficiency.

Evaluation

Perfect competition is analytically useful, but many real markets do not meet its assumptions. Product differentiation, imperfect information, and barriers to entry mean the model works best as a benchmark, not as a literal description of most industries.

06

Monopoly and the Meaning of Monopoly Power

A monopoly is formally a market with one firm, but the lecture is careful here: whether a firm counts as a monopolist depends on how narrowly the market is defined. A train operator may be the only rail provider on one route but still faces competition from cars, coaches, or air travel.

That means monopoly is not just about counting firms. It is about the closeness of substitutes and the extent to which consumers can switch away.

Practical definition

Monopoly power is strongest when substitutes are weak and barriers to entry are high. The firm then faces a relatively inelastic demand curve and gains real control over price.

Unlike the perfectly competitive firm, the monopolist faces the whole market demand curve. That curve is downward sloping, so selling more requires a lower price. As a result, MR lies below AR.

07

Barriers to Entry: Why Monopoly Can Survive

Without entry barriers, high profit would attract rivals and the monopoly position would collapse. The lecture groups barriers into structural, strategic, and legal forms.

Type Barrier Why it matters
Structural Economies of scale Large incumbent firms may have much lower average costs than entrants, creating natural monopoly conditions.
Structural Economies of scope Multi-product firms can spread costs across products and undercut specialists.
Structural Brand loyalty / product differentiation Consumers are reluctant to switch to unknown entrants.
Strategic Lower incumbent costs Established firms often have supplier links, experience, and cheaper finance.
Strategic Control of inputs or outlets Exclusive contracts or vertical integration can lock rivals out.
Legal Patents, licences, tariffs Law can directly protect the incumbent from competition.
Strategic Threat of takeover Potential entrants may stay out if they expect to be bought out or crushed quickly.

The lecture’s multiple-choice example is useful: an upward-sloping long-run average cost curve would not normally be described as a barrier to entry. The others clearly can be.

08

Monopoly Equilibrium: Price-Making with Constraints

The monopolist is a price maker, but not an all-powerful one. It cannot choose any price it likes because it is still constrained by the market demand curve. Raising price reduces quantity sold.

Rule

The monopolist maximises profit where MR = MC, then uses the demand curve to read off the price.

This is the standard contrast with perfect competition:

  • Perfect competition: price is given first, then output is chosen.
  • Monopoly: output is chosen where MR = MC, then price is taken from AR / demand.
Monopoly Diagram Logic
  • AR = demand slopes downward.
  • MR lies below AR.
  • Set MR = MC to get equilibrium quantity.
  • Move vertically to the AR curve to find the profit-maximising price.

Compared with perfect competition, monopoly usually means higher price, lower output, and weaker pressure to keep costs down. That is the starting point for standard welfare evaluation, even though those welfare diagrams are covered more explicitly elsewhere in the module.

09

Contestable Markets: When the Threat of Entry Matters

The lecture does not stop at actual market concentration. It introduces contestable market theory, which argues that the threat of entry can discipline firms even when only one firm is currently operating.

The university catering example captures the intuition: a campus cafe may appear to be a monopoly, but if the university can easily replace it with another contractor, prices and service quality remain under pressure.

Perfect contestability

A market is perfectly contestable when entry and exit costs are zero. In that case, firms must behave competitively or risk rapid entry by rivals.

This theory is particularly important for natural monopolies. Even if only one firm can efficiently operate at minimum efficient scale, that firm may still price cautiously if entry and exit are easy enough.

Evaluation

Contestability is a useful corrective to simple concentration measures, but the zero-cost entry and exit condition is very strong. In many real industries, sunk costs, legal barriers, and brand loyalty mean the threat of entry is weaker than the theory assumes.

10

Exam Questions and Model Answers

Exam Practice 6 marks

Explain why a perfectly competitive firm faces a horizontal demand curve.

Model answer

In perfect competition there are many firms selling an identical product, and each firm is very small relative to total market output. As a result, one firm cannot influence the market price. If it charges above the ruling price, consumers switch to identical rivals and the firm loses all sales. If it charges below the market price, it can sell its output but gives up revenue unnecessarily. Therefore the firm accepts the market price and faces a perfectly elastic, horizontal demand curve where price, average revenue, and marginal revenue are equal.

Exam Practice 8 marks

Explain how supernormal profits are eliminated in the long run under perfect competition.

Model answer

Supernormal profits attract new firms because entry is free in perfect competition. As new firms enter, industry supply shifts to the right, causing the market price to fall. Since each firm is a price taker, its average revenue falls with the market price. This process continues until price just equals average cost at the profit-maximising output, leaving firms with only normal profit. At that point there is no incentive for further entry, so long-run equilibrium is restored.

Exam Practice 10 marks

Assess whether a monopoly will always behave badly for consumers.

Model answer

A monopoly often charges a higher price and produces less output than a competitive industry because it faces a downward-sloping demand curve and maximises profit where MR = MC. High barriers to entry also weaken competitive pressure. However, monopoly does not automatically imply poor outcomes in every case. A natural monopoly may exploit major economies of scale, allowing one firm to supply the market at lower average cost than multiple firms. In addition, if the market is contestable, the threat of entry may force the monopolist to price more competitively. A balanced answer should therefore distinguish between monopoly structure and monopoly behaviour, and should evaluate how strong the barriers, substitutes, and regulatory constraints actually are.

11

Evaluation and Common Mistakes

Common mistake

Writing that a monopoly can set any price it wants. It cannot. The demand curve still constrains the firm, and higher prices reduce quantity demanded.

Confusing the short run with the long run in perfect competition. Supernormal profit can exist in the short run but is competed away in the long run.

Defining monopoly only by “one firm” without discussing market definition and outside substitutes.

What strong answers do

Strong answers move beyond listing assumptions. They explain how assumptions shape the demand curve faced by the firm, how that changes the pricing rule, and how entry conditions determine whether profit persists.

Where evaluation is needed, the strongest route is usually to contrast theoretical models with real markets: perfect competition is a benchmark, monopoly power depends on market definition, and contestability depends on how easy entry really is.
