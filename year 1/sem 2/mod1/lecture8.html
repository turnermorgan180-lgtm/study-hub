Key Definitions

Terms to Know Cold

Imperfect competition
Any market structure where at least one firm has some price-setting power and firms are not pure price takers.
Monopolistic competition
A market with many firms, differentiated products, and relatively free entry and exit.
Product differentiation
Real or perceived differences between rival products, often created by branding, quality, design, or location.
Abnormal profit
Profit above normal profit; total revenue exceeds total cost including opportunity cost.
Normal profit
The minimum profit needed to keep a firm in the industry in the long run.
Oligopoly
A market dominated by a few large firms whose decisions are mutually interdependent.
Interdependence
A situation where one firm must consider likely rival reactions when setting price, output, or advertising.
Collusion
Cooperation between rival firms to raise joint profit, often by restricting output or coordinating price.
Cartel
A formal collusive agreement among firms, usually involving quotas, prices, or market sharing.
Tacit collusion
Collusion achieved without an explicit written agreement, often through signalling or repeated interaction.
01

The Spectrum of Imperfect Competition

Lecture 8 is not about one single market structure. It covers the range between the competitive ideal and outright monopoly. The key point is that many real-world firms are neither powerless price takers nor pure monopolists. They operate in markets where some market power exists, but it is limited by substitutes, entry, and rival reaction.

Structure Number of firms Control over price Main strategic issue
Perfect competition Very many None No individual firm power
Monopolistic competition Many Limited Differentiation and entry
Oligopoly Few Significant but constrained Interdependence and collusion
Monopoly One High Profit versus regulation
Exam rule

When comparing market structures, do not stop at firm numbers. Always link number of firms, product differentiation, entry barriers, and pricing power to the resulting behaviour and efficiency outcome.

02

Monopolistic Competition

Monopolistic competition combines competition with a degree of monopoly power. There are many sellers, but products are differentiated, so each firm faces a downward-sloping demand curve rather than a perfectly elastic one. A coffee shop, local restaurant, hairdresser, or clothing brand may have some pricing power because customers perceive differences.

Main characteristics

  • Many firms: no single seller dominates the whole market.
  • Differentiated products: firms compete on brand, quality, design, location, or service.
  • Some price-making power: each firm can raise or lower price to some degree without losing every customer.
  • Relatively low barriers to entry: if firms earn abnormal profit, new firms can enter.
  • Heavy non-price competition: advertising and branding are often central.

The reason demand is downward sloping is that the product is not identical to rivals' products. But it is still fairly elastic because many close substitutes exist. That means firms have market power, but only in a weak form.

Exam insight

Do not describe monopolistic competition as “monopoly with many firms.” The better wording is that firms have mini-monopoly power over their own differentiated variety, but not over the whole industry.

03

Short-Run and Long-Run Profit under Monopolistic Competition

In the short run, a firm under monopolistic competition behaves like a profit maximiser facing a downward-sloping demand curve. It chooses output where MR = MC, then charges the price shown by the demand curve at that output. This can generate abnormal profit if average revenue exceeds average total cost.

How to describe the standard diagram
  • Draw downward-sloping AR and lower MR.
  • Draw upward-sloping MC and U-shaped ATC.
  • Profit-maximising output is where MR = MC.
  • Price is read from the AR curve above that output.
  • If price exceeds ATC, the shaded rectangle is abnormal profit.

In the long run, the story changes because entry is relatively easy. Abnormal profit attracts new firms. As new entrants offer similar differentiated products, each existing firm loses some customers. Its demand curve shifts leftward and becomes more elastic. Entry continues until abnormal profit is competed away.

Long-run result

New entry shifts each firm's AR and MR leftwards until only normal profit remains. The firm still chooses output where MR = MC, but now AR is just tangent to ATC at the equilibrium output-price combination.

Common mistake

Students often say “entry shifts market demand left.” More accurate: industry supply rises, and each existing firm's own demand curve shifts left because customers are spread across more sellers.

The long-run outcome is important for evaluation. Firms usually do not produce at the minimum point of average cost, so excess capacity exists. In that sense, monopolistic competition is less productively efficient than perfect competition. But consumers gain from variety, innovation, and choice.

04

Oligopoly and Interdependence

Oligopoly exists where a small number of large firms dominate the market. The defining feature is interdependence. Because there are only a few major players, each firm knows that its own actions will provoke responses from rivals. A price cut by one supermarket chain, airline, or mobile provider is unlikely to be ignored by the others.

Typical features

  • Few large firms: each has a noticeable market share.
  • Barriers to entry: scale economies, branding, patents, or strategic behaviour keep rivals out.
  • Strategic behaviour: firms must predict reactions before acting.
  • Price rigidity is possible: firms may avoid price changes if retaliation is expected.
  • Non-price competition matters: branding, advertising, loyalty schemes, quality, and service become central.

Unlike monopoly or monopolistic competition, there is no single universal oligopoly model that always applies. Outcomes depend on whether firms compete aggressively, quietly follow one another, or actively collude. That uncertainty is exactly why game theory follows in Lecture 9.

Core idea

In oligopoly, profit maximisation is harder because a firm's optimal choice depends not only on demand and cost, but also on expected rival behaviour.

05

Competition versus Collusion

Oligopolistic firms face a strategic choice. They can compete, trying to win customers through price cuts or aggressive advertising, or they can collude, seeking to soften competition and raise joint profits. From the firms' viewpoint, collusion is attractive because price wars damage profit for everyone.

Behaviour Likely output Likely price Main effect
Competition Higher Lower Consumers gain more
Collusion Restricted Higher Firms gain more

Economically, collusion tries to make the industry behave more like a monopoly. If the group can restrict output and keep members disciplined, joint profit rises. The problem is that every member also has an incentive to cheat by secretly cutting price or exceeding its quota.

Evaluation

Collusion is individually fragile even when it is jointly profitable. The tension between group interest and individual incentive to cheat is the key analytical point and links directly to prisoner’s-dilemma logic.

06

Cartels and Tacit Collusion

A cartel is a formal collusive agreement. Firms may agree prices, output quotas, market shares, or territorial division. If it works, the cartel can raise price above the competitive level and earn monopoly-style profit. The problem is enforcement. Each member wants others to stick to the agreement while it quietly undercuts them.

Tacit collusion

Not all collusion is explicit. Firms may reach similar outcomes without signing a formal agreement. This is tacit collusion. It can happen through price leadership, repeated interaction, stable expectations, or public signalling. The practical effect can still be weak competition, even if proving illegality is harder.

1
Firms recognise mutual dependence. Aggressive rivalry lowers everyone’s profit.
2
They coordinate directly or indirectly. The aim is to avoid price wars.
3
Output is restricted. Relative to competition, less is supplied.
4
Prices remain higher. Profit rises, but consumer surplus falls.
Common mistake

Do not claim tacit collusion is always legal or always illegal. The safer exam point is that explicit cartels are easier to classify and prosecute, while tacit collusion is harder to detect and prove.

07

Factors Favouring Collusion

Cartels and tacit collusion do not always succeed. They are more likely to work when market conditions make monitoring and discipline easier.

Factor Why it helps collusion
Few firms Easier to communicate, observe, and monitor each member.
Similar costs and products Makes it easier to agree on price and quota arrangements.
Stable demand Reduces uncertainty, so cheating is easier to detect.
High barriers to entry Stops outside firms from being attracted by high prices.
Transparent prices Allows firms to identify undercutting quickly.
Repeated interaction Supports punishment strategies against defectors.

By contrast, collusion is less likely to hold when firms are numerous, costs differ widely, demand is volatile, or entry is easy. In those conditions it is harder to distinguish cheating from normal market fluctuation, and harder to keep the agreement intact.

08

Oligopoly and the Consumer: Downsides and Possible Benefits

The lecture does not present oligopoly as automatically good or automatically bad. The default criticism is that weak competition can mean higher prices, restricted output, less choice, and possible allocative inefficiency. If oligopolists collude, the market can move closer to monopoly behaviour and generate deadweight welfare loss.

The downsides

  • Higher prices: especially where collusion or coordinated behaviour exists.
  • Lower output: firms restrict supply relative to a more competitive benchmark.
  • Reduced consumer surplus: some surplus is transferred to producers and some is lost entirely.
  • Potential X-inefficiency: weak competitive pressure may reduce cost discipline.
  • Barriers to entry: consumers may face fewer alternatives over time.

Potential benefits

  • Economies of scale: large firms may achieve lower unit costs.
  • Research and development: large profits may fund innovation.
  • Dynamic efficiency: firms with market power may invest heavily in new technology, design, or branding.
  • Price stability: some oligopolies avoid constant price wars, which can reduce uncertainty.
Balanced judgement

The strongest evaluation is conditional: oligopoly is harmful when market power is used to restrict competition, but it may deliver benefits when scale, innovation, and long-run investment outweigh static inefficiency.

09

Exam Questions and Model Answers

Section A Practice 6 marks

Explain why abnormal profit under monopolistic competition tends to disappear in the long run.

Model answer

In monopolistic competition, firms may earn abnormal profit in the short run because they face downward-sloping demand and choose output where MR = MC. However, barriers to entry are low. Abnormal profit attracts new firms into the market. Entry increases the number of close substitutes, so the demand curve facing each existing firm shifts left and becomes more elastic. As a result, price and sales fall for each firm. This process continues until only normal profit remains, where average revenue just covers average total cost.

Section B Practice 10 marks

Discuss whether oligopoly is likely to benefit or harm consumers.

Model answer structure

Begin by defining oligopoly as a market dominated by a few interdependent firms. Explain the consumer harm case first: firms may collude explicitly or tacitly, restrict output, and keep prices above competitive levels. This reduces consumer surplus and can create deadweight welfare loss.

Then add the counterargument. Large firms may exploit economies of scale, fund R&D, and innovate more than fragmented competitive industries. Consumers may therefore benefit from lower long-run costs, better technology, and more stable supply.

Conclude conditionally: oligopoly harms consumers when market power is used anti-competitively, but can benefit them when scale and innovation are passed on through lower costs or better products.

MCQ Logic Fast recall

As new firms enter a monopolistically competitive industry, what happens to the demand curve facing each existing firm?

Best answer

It shifts leftward and becomes more elastic, because each firm now faces more close substitutes and a smaller share of the market.

10

Evaluation and Common Mistakes

Common mistake

Do not confuse monopolistic competition with oligopoly. The former has many firms and low entry barriers; the latter has few dominant firms and strategic interdependence.

Do not write “oligopoly means firms always collude.” They may collude, but they may also compete aggressively.

Do not say “abnormal profit continues forever” under monopolistic competition. That ignores the role of entry.

What pushes answers up a grade

Top answers move beyond description. They compare short-run and long-run outcomes, distinguish explicit from tacit collusion, and evaluate consumer effects using both static efficiency and dynamic efficiency.

If you are asked to assess collusion, always include the idea that collusion may be profitable collectively but unstable individually because firms have incentives to cheat.

One-line summary

Monopolistic competition explains how entry erodes abnormal profit; oligopoly explains how interdependence and possible collusion shape profit, prices, and welfare.
