Key Definitions

Terms to Know Cold

Business cycle
Fluctuations in real output around the long-run trend.
Output gap
The difference between actual output and potential output.
Demand-pull inflation
Inflation caused by excessive aggregate demand.
Cost-push inflation
Inflation caused by rising production costs or negative supply shocks.
Cyclical unemployment
Joblessness caused by weak aggregate demand over the cycle.
Structural unemployment
Mismatch between worker skills or location and available jobs.
Part 01

Business Activity Moves Through the Cycle

Economic activity does not grow smoothly. Periods of expansion can be followed by slowdown or recession, then recovery. For firms this affects sales, confidence, inventory decisions, hiring, and investment. For macroeconomics it matters because unstable output creates unemployment when demand is weak and inflation pressure when demand is excessive.

Core rule

When actual output falls below potential output, the economy has a negative output gap. That is usually associated with spare capacity and cyclical unemployment.

Part 02

Unemployment Is Not One Single Problem

Economists separate unemployment into types because the policy response depends on the cause. Cyclical unemployment reflects insufficient aggregate demand. Structural unemployment reflects mismatch. Frictional unemployment arises while workers move between jobs. Seasonal unemployment reflects predictable annual patterns.

Part 03

Inflation Can Come From Demand or Supply

Demand-pull inflation happens when aggregate demand grows faster than the economy's productive capacity. Cost-push inflation happens when costs rise, such as wages, imported energy, or taxes on production. Exam answers need to distinguish these because the AD/AS diagram shifts are different.

Common mistake

Do not describe all inflation as “too much money chasing too few goods.” That fits demand-pull inflation better than cost-push inflation.

Part 04

Aggregate Demand and Why It Shifts

Aggregate demand is total planned spending on domestic output: consumption, investment, government spending, and net exports. It shifts when interest rates change, confidence changes, fiscal policy changes, or foreign demand changes.

  • Lower interest rates tend to raise consumption and investment.
  • Higher confidence can increase private spending.
  • A depreciation can improve net exports.
  • Tighter fiscal policy can reduce aggregate demand.
Part 05

Aggregate Supply and the Inflation Side of the Model

Short-run aggregate supply can shift left if production costs rise or supply chains are disrupted. That reduces output and raises the price level at the same time. Long-run aggregate supply reflects productive capacity and can shift right with better productivity, investment, or labour-force growth.

In diagram terms
  • A rightward shift in AD raises output and the price level in the short run.
  • A leftward shift in SRAS lowers output and raises the price level.
  • A rightward shift in LRAS raises potential output and reduces inflation pressure over time.
Part 06

How to Use AD/AS in an Exam

1
Name the shock. For example, lower interest rates, higher oil prices, or a fiscal expansion.
2
Shift the correct curve. AD, SRAS, or LRAS.
3
State the new outcome. Explain output, employment, and the price level.
4
Evaluate. Bring in time lags, expectations, or conflicting policy objectives.
Exam insight

The stronger answer always links a fall in output to higher cyclical unemployment. Do not treat labour-market outcomes as an optional afterthought.

Part 07

Trade-Offs, Policy and Evaluation

Stabilising the economy is difficult because policies that reduce inflation may weaken growth, while policies that lift demand may increase inflation pressure. Expectations matter as well: if firms and workers expect persistent inflation, wage and price setting can keep inflation going.

The best evaluation therefore recognises that there is no universal policy fix. Demand-side tools work best for cyclical weakness, while supply-side reforms matter more for structural unemployment and long-run inflation control.

Part 08

Exam Practice

Section B Style Prompt15 marks

Using an AD/AS diagram, explain how an increase in oil prices could affect unemployment and inflation.

Classify the shock as cost-push inflation and shift SRAS left. Explain that the price level rises while output falls, which increases cyclical unemployment. Then evaluate by discussing temporary versus persistent shocks, wage responses, and possible policy trade-offs between inflation control and output stabilisation.
