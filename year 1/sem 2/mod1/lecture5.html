Key Definitions

Terms to Know Cold

Opportunity cost
The value of the next best alternative foregone when a choice is made.
Sunk cost
A past cost that cannot be recovered and should not affect the current decision.
Short run
A period in which at least one factor of production is fixed.
Long run
A period in which all factors of production are variable.
Total physical product
The total output produced from a given quantity of variable input.
Marginal product
The extra output gained from employing one more unit of the variable input.
Fixed cost
Cost that does not vary with output in the short run, such as rent on premises.
Variable cost

Marginal cost

01

Opportunity Cost and Relevant Cost

The lecture opens with the economic meaning of cost. In economics, cost is not just money actually paid out. The deeper idea is opportunity cost: what must be given up in order to use resources in one way rather than another. If a firm uses a machine to produce product X, the cost is not only maintenance or electricity. It is also the value of the best alternative use sacrificed.

Core rule

Relevant costs are future costs that change with the decision. Irrelevant costs are costs that do not change with the decision, especially sunk costs.

This distinction matters because firms often make poor decisions when they let past expenditure distort current choices. If a machine has already been bought, the original purchase price is sunk. The relevant economic cost of using it for one more year is the value of the next best alternative, such as selling it or using it elsewhere.

Type of cost Should it affect the current decision? Reason
Opportunity cost Yes It captures the real sacrifice of choosing one action over another.
Sunk cost No It has already been incurred and cannot be altered now.
Avoidable future cost Yes It changes if the decision changes.
Common mistake

Do not define opportunity cost as "the price paid." Price is only one possible cost. Opportunity cost is the next best alternative forgone, whether or not money changes hands.

02

Short Run Versus Long Run

The distinction between short run and long run is conceptual, not calendar-based. The short run is any period in which at least one factor is fixed. The long run is the period in which the firm can vary all factors, including plant size, machinery, and organisational structure.

  • Short run: some inputs fixed, so output adjustment mainly comes from changing variable inputs such as labour.
  • Long run: all inputs variable, so the firm can redesign its whole production process.
  • Implication: short-run cost curves differ from long-run cost curves because the firm’s flexibility is very different.
Exam insight

When asked why short-run and long-run costs differ, the mark-winning phrase is that the long run allows full adjustment of all factors of production. Avoid saying only that the long run is "a long time."

03

The Short-Run Production Function

In the short run, output depends on combining at least one fixed factor with more units of a variable factor. The lecture sequence on short-run production implies a standard production function story: as more labour is added to a fixed plant, total output first rises at an increasing rate, then at a diminishing rate, and may eventually stop growing or even fall.

Useful relationships

Marginal product (MP) is the change in output from one more unit of variable input.
Average product (AP) is output per unit of variable input.

1
Early stage: specialisation and division of labour can raise output quickly.
2
Middle stage: output still rises, but each extra worker adds less than before.
3
Late stage: congestion and overcrowding can cause marginal product to fall toward zero or below zero.

This is why the total physical product curve changes slope: the slope of TPP is the marginal product. Once MP starts falling, the TPP curve becomes flatter. If MP becomes zero, TPP reaches its maximum. If MP becomes negative, total output begins to fall.

Exam logic: if a question asks "When does production stop growing?", the answer is when marginal product falls to zero. After that point, extra variable input adds no extra output.
04

Diminishing Returns and Overcrowding

The lecture outline explicitly includes negative returns and overcrowding. This is the law of diminishing marginal returns: once a firm keeps adding more of a variable input to fixed inputs, the marginal product of that variable input eventually declines.

The reason is intuitive. If the size of the factory, number of machines, or floor space is fixed, each extra worker has less fixed capital to work with. Workers begin to get in each other's way. That does not mean total output immediately falls. It means output rises more slowly.

Stage What happens to MP What happens to TPP
Increasing returns MP rising TPP rises at an increasing rate
Diminishing returns MP still positive but falling TPP rises at a decreasing rate
Negative returns MP negative TPP falls
Why it matters for costs

Diminishing returns are the key reason marginal cost eventually rises in the short run. If each extra worker adds less output, the firm must use more input per extra unit of output.

05

Short-Run Cost Concepts

The lecture then moves from production to cost. In the short run, some costs are fixed because some factors are fixed. Other costs vary with output because they are tied to variable inputs.

Core formulas

TC = TFC + TVC
AFC = TFC / Q
AVC = TVC / Q
ATC = TC / Q = AFC + AVC
MC = ΔTC / ΔQ

Fixed cost

Fixed cost does not change with output in the short run. If a furniture manufacturer pays rent on a workshop, that rent is paid whether output is zero or 100 tables. This is why the lecture includes a fixed-cost multiple-choice question: students must recognise that fixed cost is linked to the existence of fixed factors, not to whether a bill is paid weekly or monthly.

Variable cost

Variable cost changes with output. Extra labour, timber, electricity linked to production, or packaging costs are standard examples. Once output rises, variable costs rise too.

Common mistake

Do not say wages are always variable or always fixed. Salaried management may be fixed in the short run, while hourly production labour is typically variable. The classification depends on whether the cost changes with output in the period being analysed.

06

Total, Average, and Marginal Cost Curves

The structure of cost curves comes directly from the production logic above. Total fixed cost is constant. Total variable cost rises with output, usually first at a decreasing rate and then at an increasing rate as diminishing returns dominate. Total cost is simply the vertical sum of total fixed and total variable cost.

Average cost curves

Average fixed cost falls continuously as output rises because the same fixed cost is spread over more units. Average variable cost and average total cost are usually U-shaped. At low output, specialisation and better use of plant push average costs down. At higher output, diminishing returns push them up.

Marginal cost

Marginal cost is the cost of one extra unit. Because marginal product first rises and then falls, marginal cost typically first falls and then rises. When each extra worker adds a lot of output, the extra unit is cheap to produce. When each extra worker adds little output, the extra unit becomes expensive.

Critical relationship: the MC curve cuts AVC and ATC at their minimum points. If marginal cost is below an average, it pulls the average down. If marginal cost is above an average, it pulls the average up.
Curve Typical shape Main reason
AFC Continuously downward sloping Fixed cost is spread over more units
AVC U-shaped Initially better use of variable inputs, later diminishing returns
ATC U-shaped AVC behaviour plus falling AFC
MC U-shaped and steeper later Inverse of marginal product behaviour
07

Production in the Long Run

Once the analysis moves to the long run, the firm is no longer constrained by fixed plant. It can choose its scale of operation, adopt new machinery, reorganise labour, relocate, or redesign the entire production process. This makes long-run production fundamentally different from short-run production.

In the long run the focus shifts away from overcrowding around a fixed plant and toward whether bigger scale lowers or raises unit cost. That is why the lecture transitions from long-run production directly into economies of scale.

Exam insight

If asked how a firm responds to persistent high demand, the strongest answer is often long-run rather than short-run: it may expand plant size rather than keep pushing labour into an already crowded fixed facility.

08

Economies and Diseconomies of Scale

Economies of scale exist when expanding output reduces long-run average cost. The lecture titles explicitly frame this as Why Bigger can be Better. The reason is that larger firms can spread costs, specialise, negotiate better, and use more efficient technology.

Typical internal economies of scale

  • Technical economies: larger machines or production systems may be more efficient.
  • Managerial economies: specialist managers can improve coordination and productivity.
  • Purchasing economies: bulk buying can lower input prices.
  • Financial economies: large firms may borrow more cheaply.
  • Marketing economies: advertising and distribution costs can be spread over more output.

Diseconomies of scale begin when the firm becomes so large that coordination problems, bureaucracy, weak communication, and monitoring difficulties raise average costs. The lecture also mentions external factors, which points to the distinction between internal scale effects inside the firm and wider industry conditions outside it.

Case Effect on LRAC Main intuition
Economies of scale LRAC falls Bigger scale improves efficiency
Constant returns to scale LRAC flat Unit cost roughly unchanged
Diseconomies of scale LRAC rises Complexity and coordination costs dominate
09

Long-Run Average Cost and the Envelope Idea

The LRAC curve shows the lowest possible average cost of producing each output when the firm can choose the optimal plant size. In textbook terms, LRAC is the envelope of a series of short-run average cost curves. Each SRAC corresponds to a different plant size. The firm chooses the plant that minimises unit cost for the required output.

Interpretation

LRAC does not show one short-run plant being stretched forever. It shows the lowest attainable average cost once the firm is free to move between different short-run plants.

That is why LRAC is often flatter and smoother than SRAC. The firm can avoid producing every quantity on a suboptimal plant. At low output it may use a small plant. At medium output it may switch to a larger, more efficient plant. At very high output, diseconomies of scale may eventually push LRAC upward again.

Exam description of the diagram: draw several U-shaped SRAC curves, then a smoother LRAC tangent to them from below. Explain that each tangency point represents the cheapest plant size for that output level.
10

Exam Questions and Model Answers

Exam Practice 6 marks

Explain why marginal cost rises in the short run after a certain point.

Model answer

In the short run at least one factor of production is fixed, such as plant size. As the firm adds more units of the variable factor, for example labour, total output initially rises quickly because of specialisation. However, beyond a certain point the law of diminishing marginal returns sets in, so each additional worker adds less extra output because the fixed factor becomes crowded.

Since each extra unit of output now requires relatively more variable input, the cost of producing one more unit rises. Therefore marginal cost rises. The key link is that falling marginal product implies rising marginal cost.

Exam Practice 8 marks

Distinguish between fixed cost, variable cost, and sunk cost, and explain why the distinction matters for decision-making.

Model answer

Fixed costs do not vary with output in the short run, because they are linked to fixed factors such as rent on a factory. Variable costs do vary with output, because they depend on variable inputs such as production labour or raw materials. Sunk costs are past costs that have already been incurred and cannot be recovered.

The distinction matters because firms should base decisions on relevant future costs, not on sunk costs. For example, if a machine has already been bought, the purchase price is sunk. The relevant cost of using it now is its opportunity cost, such as what could be earned by selling it or using it elsewhere. Confusing sunk cost with relevant cost leads to poor economic decisions.

Exam Practice 10 marks

Explain the causes of economies of scale and evaluate whether becoming larger always lowers average cost.

Model answer

Economies of scale occur when long-run average cost falls as output increases. Causes include technical economies from more efficient machinery, managerial economies from specialist staff, purchasing economies from bulk buying, financial economies from cheaper access to funds, and marketing economies from spreading promotional costs over larger output.

However, becoming larger does not always lower average cost. Beyond some point the firm may suffer diseconomies of scale. Communication becomes harder, bureaucracy expands, monitoring weakens, and decision-making slows down. External factors such as congestion or industry-wide input shortages may also push costs up. Therefore scale can reduce cost, but only up to the point where organisational complexity begins to dominate.

11

Evaluation and Common Mistakes

Evaluation points worth using

Evaluation

Short-run cost theory is stylised. Real firms may automate, redesign jobs, outsource, or improve workflow before diminishing returns become severe.

Economies of scale are not guaranteed. Some firms stay deliberately small because flexibility, quality control, or niche positioning matters more than mass production.

Accounting cost and economic cost are not the same. Strong answers can improve by bringing in opportunity cost where appropriate.

Frequent exam mistakes

Avoid this

Do not confuse fixed cost with sunk cost. A fixed cost can still be relevant if it will be incurred in the future and changes across choices; a sunk cost is always past and irrecoverable.

Do not say diminishing returns mean total output falls immediately. Diminishing returns begin when marginal product starts to fall, even if total output is still rising.

Do not say MC cuts ATC because the curves "meet there." The reason is analytical: marginal values pull averages up or down.

Do not discuss long-run economies of scale using short-run overcrowding logic. Keep the two frameworks separate.
