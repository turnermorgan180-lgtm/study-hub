Key Definitions

Terms to Know Cold

Game
An interaction between rational players where each player’s outcome depends on the decisions of all players, not just their own.
Strategy
A complete plan of action telling a player what to do at every possible decision point in the game.
Payoff
The benefit a player receives from a given outcome. For firms this is typically profit; for individuals it may be utility or income.
Nash equilibrium
A set of strategies where no player can improve their payoff by changing strategy unilaterally, given what all other players are doing.
Dominant strategy
A strategy that gives the highest payoff regardless of what any other player does. A rational player will always play it if one exists.
Prisoner’s dilemma
A game where individually rational choices lead to a collectively worse outcome. The Nash equilibrium is Pareto inferior to the cooperative outcome.
Credible threat
A threat the threatening player would actually carry out, because doing so is in their own interest when the time comes. Non-credible threats can be ignored.
Grim trigger
Cooperate initially; if the rival defects even once, punish forever. Creates the strongest possible deterrent against cheating in repeated games.
Part 01

What Is a Game?

Game theory studies strategic interaction — situations where each player’s outcome depends not only on their own decision, but on everyone else’s. It is the primary tool for analysing oligopoly, where firms are few enough that each must think about how rivals will respond.

Core assumption

All players are assumed to be rational — they aim to maximise their own payoff, and they know that rivals do the same. This mutual awareness of rationality is what makes strategic thinking necessary.

Players — who makes decisions (firms, individuals). Strategies — the complete set of actions available. Payoffs — the outcome received for every possible combination of strategies.

Games are classified by timing: simultaneous (players choose at the same time, without observing rivals) vs sequential (players move in turn, later players observe earlier decisions). And by repetition: one-shot vs repeated — which dramatically changes incentives.

Part 02

Nash Equilibrium

Definition

A Nash equilibrium is a combination of strategies such that each player is choosing the best response to the other players’ strategies. No player has any incentive to deviate unilaterally.

The key word is unilaterally. Nash equilibrium does not mean both players are happy — it means neither can do better by switching alone. Players could both be better off switching simultaneously (a Pareto improvement), but no individual has the incentive to go first.

How to find Nash equilibria — the method

1
Find Player 1’s best response to each of Player 2’s strategies. Mark the higher payoff in each column.
2
Find Player 2’s best response to each of Player 1’s strategies. Mark the higher payoff in each row.
3
Any cell where both players’ best responses coincide is a Nash equilibrium. There may be zero, one, or multiple Nash equilibria.
Economic intuition

Think of Nash equilibrium as a stable resting point. If both players are at their Nash strategies, neither has reason to move. Rational players will tend toward it through anticipating each other’s behaviour. Named after John Nash, who proved every finite game has at least one Nash equilibrium.

Part 03

Dominant Strategies

Definition

A dominant strategy gives the highest payoff regardless of what any rival does. A rational player will always play their dominant strategy — it eliminates the need to predict the rival’s move.

Example — pricing game (from your lecture)

Firm X and Firm Y each choose between £2.00 or £1.80. Payoffs (£m):

Payoff matrix — (Firm X profit, Firm Y profit) in £m
Firm Y: £2.00Firm Y: £1.80
Firm X: £2.00(10, 10) ← Pareto optimal(6, 12)
Firm X: £1.80(12, 6)(8, 8) ← Nash equilibrium

£1.80 is the dominant strategy for both firms. If Y charges £2.00: X prefers £1.80 (£12m > £10m). If Y charges £1.80: X prefers £1.80 (£8m > £6m). The logic is symmetric for Y. Both charge £1.80 → Nash equilibrium of (£8m, £8m). Yet both charging £2.00 yields (£10m, £10m) — better for both. This is the prisoner’s dilemma in a business context.

Part 04

The Prisoner’s Dilemma

The prisoner’s dilemma is the most important result in game theory. Individually rational behaviour produces a collectively irrational outcome — with implications for oligopoly, environmental policy, arms races, and beyond.

Nigel and Amanda (your lecture example)

Prisoner’s dilemma — sentences in years (Nigel, Amanda)
Amanda: SilentAmanda: Confess
Nigel: Silent(1yr, 1yr) ← Pareto optimal(10yrs, 3mths)
Nigel: Confess(3mths, 10yrs)(3yrs, 3yrs) ← Nash equilibrium

Confessing is the dominant strategy for both. Each prisoner reasons: whatever the other does, I am better off confessing. Both confess → (3yrs, 3yrs). Yet both staying silent yields (1yr, 1yr) — better for both. Individually rational play is collectively self-defeating.

Application to oligopoly

Replace confess/silent with undercut/maintain price. Each firm has a dominant strategy to undercut. Both undercutting produces a low-price Nash equilibrium. Both maintaining a high price would be better for both — but each fears the rival will undercut while they hold firm. This is why oligopoly prices tend toward competitive levels even without antitrust enforcement.

Pareto optimality vs Nash equilibrium

The Pareto optimal outcome (both silent; both price high) is not a Nash equilibrium — it requires trust that individually rational players cannot sustain in a one-shot game. The Nash equilibrium (both confess; both undercut) is Pareto inferior. Both players could be better off, but neither will unilaterally cooperate. Always make this distinction explicit in exam answers.

Part 05

Sequential Games & Game Trees

Definition

A sequential game is one where players decide one after another, with later players able to observe earlier decisions. A game tree maps the sequence of choices and resulting payoffs. Solve using backward induction — start from the end and work backwards.

How to read and solve a game tree

1
Decision nodes show where a player chooses. The first mover’s node is on the left. Branches represent available actions.
2
Terminal nodes (end points) show payoffs as (first mover, second mover).
3
Backward induction: at each second-mover node, identify which branch gives the second mover the higher payoff — this is what they will rationally choose.
4
The first mover anticipates the second mover’s rational response and picks the branch that maximises their own payoff given those anticipated responses.

Boeing vs Airbus — your lecture example

Boeing moves first, choosing 400-seater or 500-seater. Only one firm can profitably produce each type. If both choose the same: each loses £10m.

Backward induction: If Boeing plays 400-seater → Airbus picks 500-seater (£30m > −£10m). If Boeing plays 500-seater → Airbus picks 400-seater (£50m > −£10m). Boeing anticipates: 400-seater → earns £50m; 500-seater → earns £30m. Boeing picks 400-seater. Nash equilibrium: (400-seater, 500-seater) with payoffs (£50m, £30m).

Part 06

First-Mover Advantage

Definition

First-mover advantage occurs when acting first in a sequential game secures a more favourable outcome. By committing early, the first mover shapes the choice set available to rivals and forces them into a less profitable best response.

In Boeing/Airbus, Boeing captures the more profitable 400-seater market (£50m) because it moves first. Airbus is forced to accept £30m. First-mover advantage depends on: speed of R&D, ability to build capacity quickly, securing key resources, and establishing brand loyalty before rivals arrive.

Evaluation — first vs second mover

Being first is not always advantageous. Sometimes a second-mover advantage exists — observing the rival’s move and responding optimally can be better, especially when technology is uncertain or the first mover must bear high development costs. Google was not the first search engine; Facebook was not the first social network. The second mover learned from the pioneer’s mistakes and executed better.

Part 07

Credible Threats & Promises

Definition

A credible threat is one the threatening player would actually carry out, because doing so is in their own interest. A non-credible threat would not be carried out — the threatened player can safely ignore it.

The credibility test: look ahead in the game tree. At the node where the threat must be executed, would the threatening player actually benefit from following through? If yes → credible. If no → non-credible.

New-Entrant vs Incumbent (2022–23 exam, Q27)

Incumbent threatens to fight regardless of how New-Entrant enters. If New-Entrant enters aggressively: Incumbent earns £1m (acquiesce) vs £0.75m (fight) → she prefers to acquiesce. The threat is non-credible in this branch. New-Entrant, solving by backward induction, enters aggressively, earns £2m, and correctly ignores the threat.

Making threats credible — commitment devices

Firms can make otherwise non-credible threats credible through irreversible commitments. An incumbent that visibly invests in large excess capacity is credibly threatening to flood the market — the capacity already exists and its cost is sunk, so using it costs little. A price guarantee ("we match any lower price") publicly commits the firm to fighting price cuts, making that response credible without needing to be tested.

Part 08

Repeated Games & Cooperation

The prisoner’s dilemma assumes a one-shot game. When the game is repeated — as in real oligopolies where firms interact continuously — cooperation may become sustainable, because firms can reward cooperation and punish defection over time.

The grim trigger strategy

Definition

Grim trigger: cooperate in every period as long as the rival cooperates. If the rival defects even once, switch to the Nash equilibrium (defect) forever. The permanent punishment creates the strongest possible deterrent.

It works because the prospect of losing cooperative profits permanently outweighs the one-period gain from defecting. It is most effective when: players are patient (value future profits highly), the game is expected to continue indefinitely, and defection is observable.

Tit-for-tat

A softer strategy: cooperate first, then do whatever the rival did last period. Tit-for-tat is nice (starts cooperative), retaliatory (punishes cheating immediately), forgiving (returns to cooperation if the rival does), and clear (the rival can easily understand and anticipate it).

Finitely repeated games — unravelling

If the game has a known, fixed end date, cooperation unravels. In the final round there is no future punishment, so both players defect. But anticipating this, both defect in the second-to-last round too. By backward induction, defection unravels to round one. Cooperation cannot be sustained in finitely repeated games — at least in theory.

Application — tacit collusion in oligopoly

Repeated game logic explains why oligopolies often maintain high prices without explicit illegal agreements. Each firm understands that cutting price today triggers retaliation, eliminating the short-run gain. The prospect of future price wars sustains tacit cooperation — called tacit collusion.

Evaluation — when does cooperation break down?

Cooperation is more fragile when: firms have high discount rates (prioritise short-run gains), markets are growing rapidly (temptation to grab share is high), there are many firms making monitoring difficult, demand is falling (less to lose from a price war), or there is a detectable end to the relationship.

The 2008 financial crisis illustrates this: firms facing existential pressure defected on tacit pricing agreements — cooperation is most fragile precisely when the stakes are highest.

Part 09

Exam Questions & Model Answers

Section B — 2021–22 paper, Q316 marks

Find all (pure) Nash equilibria in the payoff matrix: Red/Blue vs Apple/Pear with payoffs (3,3), (6,0), (0,6), (5,5).

Player 2: ApplePlayer 2: Pear
Player 1: Red(3, 3) ← Nash(6, 0)
Player 1: Blue(0, 6)(5, 5) Pareto optimal
Model answer

Player 1’s best responses: If P2 plays Apple → P1 gets 3 (Red) or 0 (Blue) → Red. If P2 plays Pear → P1 gets 6 (Red) or 5 (Blue) → Red. Red is P1’s dominant strategy.

Player 2’s best responses: If P1 plays Red → P2 gets 3 (Apple) or 0 (Pear) → Apple. If P1 plays Blue → P2 gets 6 (Apple) or 5 (Pear) → Apple. Apple is P2’s dominant strategy.

Nash equilibrium: (Red, Apple) with payoff (3, 3). This is the unique pure Nash equilibrium. (Blue, Pear) with payoff (5, 5) is Pareto superior but not a Nash equilibrium — either player would deviate to Red/Apple to improve their individual payoff. This illustrates the prisoner’s dilemma: individually rational play produces a collectively suboptimal outcome.

Section B — 2022–23 paper, Q27 (parts b & c)8 marks

Mr New-Entrant enters aggressively: payoffs (£2m, £1m) if Incumbent acquiesces; (£0.75m, £0.75m) if fights. Enters passively: (£0.5m, £0.5m) if acquiesces; (£1m, £2m) if fights. (b) What is the Nash equilibrium? (c) Is Incumbent’s threat to always fight credible?

Part b — Nash equilibrium (4 marks)

Solving by backward induction. If New-Entrant enters aggressively: Incumbent earns £1m (acquiesce) vs £0.75m (fight) → Incumbent acquiesces. If New-Entrant enters passively: Incumbent earns £0.5m (acquiesce) vs £2m (fight) → Incumbent fights. New-Entrant anticipates this: aggressive → £2m; passive → £1m. New-Entrant enters aggressively.

Most likely Nash equilibrium: (Aggressive entry, Acquiesce) with payoffs (£2m, £1m).

Part c — credibility (4 marks)

Incumbent threatens to fight regardless. The credibility test: would she actually fight if entry is aggressive? If entry is aggressive: fight yields £0.75m vs acquiesce yields £1m → Incumbent prefers to acquiesce. She would not follow through. The threat is non-credible for the aggressive branch. New-Entrant correctly ignores it and enters aggressively. A rational player should not allow a non-credible threat to influence their decision.

Section A MCQ — Battle of the Sexes (2021–22, Q12)2 marks

John prefers opera with Jasmine over movies alone. Jasmine prefers movies with John over opera alone. Which is NOT a Nash equilibrium? (a) Both opera (b) Both movies (c) John to movies, Jasmine to opera (d) At least one Nash equilibrium exists.

Answer — C

If John is at movies and Jasmine is at opera (each at their individually preferred venue, alone), John would deviate — he prefers opera with Jasmine over movies alone. Since he can improve by switching unilaterally, this is not a Nash equilibrium. Both-opera (A) and both-movies (B) are Nash equilibria — at each, neither player benefits from deviating alone. Option D is trivially true. C is the incorrect statement.

Part 10

Evaluation & Common Mistakes

Evaluation — assumptions of game theory

Game theory assumes complete rationality — all players maximise their payoff and know rivals do too. In reality, bounded rationality and behavioural factors (fairness norms, reciprocity) cause players to cooperate even in one-shot games. Experimental economics consistently shows cooperation rates above the Nash prediction.

Game theory also assumes players know the full payoff matrix. In practice, firms rarely know rivals’ exact cost structures or profitability. Incomplete information substantially complicates the analysis — this is an active area of economic research.

Evaluation — multiple Nash equilibria

Many games have multiple Nash equilibria (Battle of the Sexes has two). Game theory cannot always predict which will be reached. Selection depends on focal points, communication, historical precedent, and social norms — factors outside the standard model. Knowing the equilibrium set is not enough to predict behaviour.

Mistake 1 — Confusing Nash equilibrium with the best outcome

Nash equilibrium is the stable outcome, not the best one. In the prisoner’s dilemma, the Nash equilibrium is worse for both players than the cooperative outcome. Always distinguish Nash equilibrium (individually stable) from Pareto optimum (collectively best).

Mistake 2 — Assuming a dominant strategy always exists

Not every game has a dominant strategy. When the best choice depends on the rival’s move, you must find Nash equilibria by checking best responses cell by cell. Never assume dominance before checking every row and column.

Mistake 3 — Applying one-shot logic to repeated games

Concluding "both players will defect" ignores whether the game is repeated. In infinitely repeated games, cooperation can be sustained through credible punishment strategies. Always establish the game’s repetition structure before drawing behavioural conclusions.

Mistake 4 — Not checking credibility at the right node

A threat sounds plausible but may be non-credible. The test is always: at the specific node where the threat must be executed, does carrying it out benefit the threatening player? Check the payoffs at that node only — do not rely on intuition or the firm’s general reputation.

Mistake 5 — Drawing game trees incorrectly

First mover’s node goes on the left. Branches represent actions, not outcomes. Payoffs go at terminal nodes on the right as (first mover, second mover). Solve right to left (backward induction), never left to right. Label every node and payoff clearly — the examiner awards marks for structure, not just the final answer.
