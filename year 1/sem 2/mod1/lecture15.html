Key Definitions

Terms to Know Cold

Balance of payments
A record of all transactions between residents of one country and the rest of the world.
Current account
Trade in goods and services, plus primary and secondary income flows.
Financial account
Cross-border movements of financial capital such as portfolio and direct investment.
Depreciation
A fall in the value of a currency in a floating exchange-rate system.
Appreciation
A rise in the value of a currency in a floating exchange-rate system.
Competitiveness
The relative ability of domestic firms to sell against foreign producers.
Part 01

The Balance of Payments Is an Organised Accounting Framework

The balance of payments records economic transactions with the rest of the world. The current account covers trade in goods and services and income flows. The financial account records capital movements such as foreign direct investment and portfolio flows. Exam questions often ask students to separate these correctly.

Core rule

A current-account deficit means the country is spending more on foreign output than foreigners are spending on its output. That gap must be financed somehow, often through financial inflows.

Part 02

Why the Current Account Moves Into Deficit or Surplus

A deficit can arise because domestic income rises faster than foreign income, because the exchange rate is overvalued, because domestic inflation erodes competitiveness, or because consumers and firms demand imports strongly. A surplus can emerge when exports are strong, import demand is weak, or the currency is relatively cheap.

Part 03

Exchange Rates Are Prices of Currencies

An exchange rate tells you how much one currency is worth in terms of another. In a floating system, the rate is largely determined by demand and supply in foreign-exchange markets. Demand for pounds comes from foreigners buying UK exports or UK financial assets. Supply of pounds comes from UK residents buying foreign goods, services, or assets.

In diagram terms
  • Draw demand for pounds downward sloping and supply upward sloping.
  • An increase in demand for UK assets shifts demand right and appreciates sterling.
  • An increase in UK demand for imports shifts supply right and depreciates sterling.
Part 04

Depreciation Versus Appreciation

A depreciation makes exports cheaper in foreign currency terms and imports more expensive in domestic currency terms. That tends to support net exports and aggregate demand, although the effect depends on elasticities and time lags. An appreciation does the reverse: it lowers import prices but can weaken export competitiveness.

1
Depreciation raises import prices. Cost-push inflation may increase.
2
Exports become relatively cheaper. Foreign demand may rise.
3
Net exports may improve. Aggregate demand and output can increase.
Exam insight

Do not assume depreciation always improves the trade balance immediately. The effect depends on price elasticity and often arrives with a lag.

Part 05

What Moves Exchange Rates?

Interest-rate expectations, inflation differentials, growth prospects, speculation, and confidence in domestic assets all matter. A higher domestic interest rate can attract financial inflows and appreciate the currency. Expected inflation can weaken demand for the currency because investors anticipate lower real returns or future depreciation.

Exam framing

The stronger answer distinguishes between trade flows and capital flows. Exchange rates can move sharply even without immediate changes in exports because financial markets respond quickly to expectations.

Part 06

Why Firms Care About Exchange Rates

Import-dependent firms face higher costs when the domestic currency depreciates. Exporters may gain competitiveness. Multinationals must also think about translation risk, sourcing decisions, and whether to hedge foreign-currency exposure. The business-economics angle is that exchange rates affect pricing, margins, and market-entry strategy.

Part 07

Evaluation and Common Pitfalls

Common mistakes

Do not confuse a depreciation in a floating system with a devaluation in a fixed or managed system.

Do not say the balance of payments is “bad” just because the current account is in deficit. The stronger answer evaluates whether the deficit is sustainable and how it is financed.

Some deficits are manageable if they finance productive investment. Some appreciations are beneficial because they reduce imported inflation. The correct judgement depends on inflation, elasticity, capital mobility, and the wider macro context.

Part 08

Exam Practice

Section B Style Prompt15 marks

Explain how a depreciation of sterling might affect UK firms and the UK economy.

Define depreciation clearly, then separate exporters from importers. Explain that export prices in foreign currency fall while import prices in pounds rise. Link this to competitiveness, inflation, and aggregate demand. Finish with evaluation using elasticity, time lags, and the possibility that imported inputs make production more expensive for domestic firms.
