Key Definitions

Terms to Know Cold

Demand
The quantity consumers are willing and able to buy at different prices over a given time period.
Effective demand
Demand backed by both willingness to buy and the purchasing power to pay.
Law of demand
Ceteris paribus, a rise in price reduces quantity demanded and a fall in price increases quantity demanded.
Movement along demand
A change in quantity demanded caused only by a change in the good’s own price.
Shift in demand
A change in demand caused by a non-price determinant such as income, tastes, or the price of related goods.
Normal good
A good for which demand rises when consumer income rises.
Inferior good
A good for which demand falls when consumer income rises.
Substitute
A rival good that can be used instead, so a higher price of one raises demand for the other.
Complement
A good consumed jointly with another, so a higher price of one reduces demand for the other.
Consumer surplus
The difference between what consumers are willing to pay and what they actually pay.
01

Why Demand Matters

Demand is the demand-side foundation of microeconomics. Firms cannot decide sensibly on output, pricing, promotion, or market entry unless they understand what drives consumers to buy. A business may be technically efficient, but if it misreads demand it will still overproduce, underprice, or fail to match what customers actually value.

Core idea

Demand is not desire on its own. It is desire backed by purchasing power. Economics is concerned with effective demand, not abstract wants with no ability to pay.

The lecture’s business logic is straightforward. Consumers face scarcity, so they choose. Firms respond to those choices. Markets then coordinate those decisions through price. That is why the consumer sits at the centre of market activity: firms survive only if they can anticipate and satisfy demand better than rivals.

Exam insight

When asked what determines sales, do not jump straight to price. Strong answers distinguish between price effects and non-price determinants such as income, tastes, demographics, expectations, and related-goods prices.

02

The Law of Demand

The demand curve slopes downward because, other things equal, lower prices make the good attractive to more consumers and encourage existing consumers to buy more. Higher prices do the reverse. This is summarised in the law of demand.

Law of demand

Ceteris paribus, price and quantity demanded are inversely related.

The phrase ceteris paribus matters. It means we hold other determinants of demand constant. If income, tastes, or the price of substitutes change at the same time, then we are no longer observing the pure effect of the good’s own price.

Why the curve slopes down

  • Diminishing marginal utility: additional units of a good usually generate less extra satisfaction than earlier units.
  • Substitution effect: when a good becomes cheaper relative to others, consumers switch toward it.
  • Income effect: a lower price increases real purchasing power, allowing more consumption.
  • Market entry effect: lower prices may bring in consumers who were previously priced out.
Common mistake

Students often say “price causes demand to fall.” More precise wording is needed: a higher price causes quantity demanded to fall. The demand curve itself only shifts when a non-price determinant changes.

03

Movements Along the Curve vs Shifts of the Curve

This is one of the highest-value distinctions in introductory microeconomics. Examiners repeatedly reward students who separate a movement along demand from a shift in demand.

Change Cause Diagram result Language to use
Extension / contraction Change in the good's own price Movement along the same demand curve Quantity demanded rises or falls
Increase / decrease in demand Change in a non-price determinant Entire demand curve shifts right or left Demand rises or falls
1
Ask what changed. If it is the good’s own price, think movement along the curve.
2
Ask whether preferences or conditions changed. If so, think shift of the whole curve.
3
Use the right terminology. “Demand increased” and “quantity demanded increased” are not interchangeable.
A fall in the price of coffee causes an extension of demand for coffee. A rise in income that makes people buy more coffee at every price causes an increase in demand for coffee.
04

Main Determinants of Demand

Non-price determinants explain why consumers may want more or less of a product even when its price has not changed. These factors are central to business strategy because firms can sometimes influence them through branding, product quality, positioning, and advertising.

Determinant If it rises or improves Typical demand effect
Income Higher purchasing power Demand rises for normal goods, falls for inferior goods
Tastes and preferences Product becomes more desirable Demand shifts right
Price of substitutes Rival product becomes dearer Demand shifts right
Price of complements Jointly used product becomes dearer Demand shifts left
Population / demographics More relevant consumers enter the market Demand usually rises
Expectations Consumers expect future price rises or shortages Current demand may rise

Normal and inferior goods

A normal good has positive income-demand relationship: higher income increases demand. An inferior good has negative income-demand relationship: as income rises, consumers switch away toward preferred alternatives. Inferior does not mean low quality; it means income-related purchasing behaviour.

Substitutes and complements

Substitutes compete for the same consumer need, such as tea and coffee. Complements are jointly consumed, such as printers and ink or cars and petrol. These cross-market links matter because one firm’s pricing can affect demand in another market.

05

Individual Demand and Market Demand

An individual demand curve shows how one consumer’s purchases respond to price. Market demand adds together all individual demands horizontally at each price. This gives the total quantity demanded in the whole market.

Aggregation rule

Market demand = horizontal sum of all individual demand curves. At each price, add quantities, not prices.

The distinction matters because market demand can change either because existing consumers alter their buying behaviour or because the number and composition of consumers changes. A growing student population, for example, can shift demand for budget housing, cafes, and public transport even if each individual’s preferences stay unchanged.

Business application

Firms care about aggregate market demand, but pricing and segmentation decisions often depend on differences across individual consumers. A product can face one broad market demand curve while still containing distinct groups with different willingness to pay.

06

Utility and Diminishing Marginal Utility

Consumer theory starts from the idea of utility: the satisfaction a consumer gains from consuming goods and services. While utility is not directly observable in practice, it is a useful way to explain why consumers make trade-offs.

Key distinction

Total utility is the satisfaction from all units consumed. Marginal utility is the extra satisfaction from one additional unit.

The standard assumption is diminishing marginal utility. The first unit of a good usually gives substantial satisfaction, but each extra unit adds less than the previous one. This helps explain why consumers will only buy additional units at lower prices.

1
Early units are highly valued. A hungry consumer values the first sandwich strongly.
2
Additional units add less satisfaction. By the third or fourth sandwich, the extra benefit is much lower.
3
Willingness to pay falls. The consumer will buy more only if price drops enough to match lower marginal utility.

This is one route to the downward-sloping demand curve. If consumers value successive units less and less, their willingness to pay for extra units also declines.

07

Income and Substitution Effects

When the price of a good changes, consumer behaviour adjusts for two related reasons. First, the good becomes relatively cheaper or more expensive compared with alternatives. Second, the consumer’s real purchasing power changes. These are the substitution and income effects.

Substitution effect

If the price of a good falls, consumers tend to substitute toward it and away from relatively more expensive alternatives. This effect reinforces the law of demand for most goods.

Income effect

If the price of a good falls, the consumer can afford more with the same money income. Real income rises. For normal goods this tends to increase quantity demanded further. For inferior goods the income effect works in the opposite direction, although usually not strongly enough to overturn the law of demand.

Normal good: substitution effect and income effect both push in the same direction after a price fall.
Inferior good: substitution effect still raises quantity demanded after a price fall, but the income effect partly offsets it.
Common mistake

Do not write that lower prices “increase money income.” They do not. They increase real income or purchasing power, because the same nominal income now buys more.

08

Consumer Surplus and Willingness to Pay

Consumer surplus measures the gain consumers receive from market exchange. Many buyers would have been willing to pay more than the market price, but they only need to pay the equilibrium price. The gap is surplus.

Definition

Consumer surplus = willingness to pay − actual price paid. On a diagram, it is the area below the demand curve and above the market price up to the quantity purchased.

This concept matters because it links demand to welfare. A fall in price usually increases consumer surplus. A successful innovation can increase consumer surplus by giving consumers more value than they expected at the price charged. Equally, taxes, shortages, or monopoly pricing may reduce it.

Why businesses should care

  • Large consumer surplus may signal scope for price discrimination.
  • Firms want to understand how much value consumers perceive relative to price.
  • Policy-makers use consumer surplus when evaluating gains and losses from market changes.
Exam insight

Consumer surplus is not the same as profit. It is a benefit to consumers, not producers. In welfare questions, keep consumer surplus and producer surplus clearly separate.

09

Special Cases and Exceptions

The law of demand is powerful, but it is not literally universal in every imaginable case. A strong student knows the general rule and can also state why unusual cases are exceptions rather than the norm.

Case Why it is unusual Exam-safe interpretation
Giffen good A strongly inferior good where the negative income effect outweighs the substitution effect Theoretical exception; rare in practice
Veblen good Higher price may itself signal prestige or exclusivity Relevant to luxury branding and status consumption
Expectation-driven demand Consumers buy more after a price rise if they expect further rises Short-run speculative behaviour, not the normal demand rule

These cases should be used carefully. In most exam questions, unless prestige goods or unusual household behaviour is explicitly mentioned, the normal downward-sloping demand assumption still applies.

10

Exam Questions and Model Answers

Section A style question 2 marks

A fall in the price of a good, holding all other influences constant, will normally cause:

A. an increase in demand.

B. a fall in demand.

C. an extension of demand.

D. a shift to the left of demand.

E. a fall in consumer surplus.

Answer

C. an extension of demand.

The good’s own price has changed, so this is a movement along the same demand curve. “Increase in demand” would only be correct if a non-price determinant shifted the whole curve rightward.

Section A style question 2 marks

If tea and coffee are substitutes, then a rise in the price of tea is most likely to:

A. reduce demand for coffee.

B. increase demand for coffee.

C. reduce quantity demanded of coffee.

D. increase supply of coffee.

E. leave the coffee market unchanged.

Answer

B. increase demand for coffee.

Because tea and coffee compete in consumption, a higher tea price makes coffee relatively more attractive. The coffee demand curve shifts right.

Section B style question 8 marks

Explain why a demand curve normally slopes downward.

Model answer

A demand curve normally slopes downward because, ceteris paribus, consumers buy more when the price is lower and less when the price is higher. One reason is diminishing marginal utility: additional units of the good give less extra satisfaction, so consumers are only willing to buy more units if price falls.

A second reason is the substitution effect. If the good becomes cheaper relative to alternatives, consumers switch toward it and away from substitutes. A third reason is the income effect. A lower price increases real purchasing power, so for most goods consumers can afford to buy more.

These forces together create an inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded, which is shown by a downward-sloping demand curve.

Section B style question 10 marks

Explain the difference between a movement along a demand curve and a shift of the demand curve.

Model answer

A movement along a demand curve happens when the product’s own price changes. If price falls, there is an extension of demand and quantity demanded increases. If price rises, there is a contraction of demand and quantity demanded falls. The demand curve itself does not move.

A shift of the demand curve happens when a non-price determinant changes. Examples include income, tastes, the price of substitutes or complements, expectations, or the number of consumers. An increase in demand means consumers want to buy more at every price, so the curve shifts right. A decrease in demand means consumers want to buy less at every price, so the curve shifts left.

This distinction matters because it changes both the wording and the diagram analysis. Price changes alter quantity demanded. Non-price changes alter demand.

11

Evaluation and Common Mistakes

Evaluation points to use in essays

Ceteris paribus is a simplification: in real markets many determinants change at once, making pure demand analysis harder.

Consumer behaviour is not perfectly rational: habits, branding, framing, and social influence can shape demand beyond standard textbook logic.

Different consumers behave differently: one market demand curve hides variation across income groups, ages, and preferences.

Expectations matter: today’s demand may respond not just to current conditions but to anticipated future prices, shortages, or income changes.

Short run vs long run: consumers may take time to adjust fully to price and income changes.

Top mistakes

1. Confusing “demand” with “quantity demanded.”

2. Forgetting that demand must be backed by ability to pay.

3. Saying an income rise increases demand for every good.

4. Mixing up substitutes and complements.

5. Defining consumer surplus as business profit.

One-line memory aid

Own price changes move you along demand.
Other influences shift demand.
The consumer buys more when the extra benefit justifies the price.
